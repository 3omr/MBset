### Q42: Grave's disease '1ry thyrotoxicosis' (Define, clinical picture, laboratory investigations, treatment)

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Graves disease is autoimmune primary hyperthyroidism caused by TSH-receptor-stimulating antibodies. Symptoms include heat intolerance, sweating, weight loss despite appetite, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, frequent stools and menstrual disturbance. Signs include diffuse goiter with bruit, tachycardia or atrial fibrillation, warm moist skin, fine tremor, lid retraction/stare and, in Graves disease, ophthalmopathy or pretibial dermopathy. TSH is suppressed with raised free T4 and/or T3; TSH-receptor antibodies support the diagnosis. If needed, uptake is diffusely increased; ultrasound shows diffuse enlargement and hypervascularity. Relieve adrenergic symptoms with a beta blocker; control hormone synthesis with methimazole (PTU is preferred in the first trimester of pregnancy). Definitive options are radioactive iodine or thyroidectomy, selected for the patient and disease; monitor thyroid function and treat resulting hypothyroidism.

### Q43: Thyrotoxicosis (causes, symptoms, specific clinical signs, Diagnosis & treatment)

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Thyrotoxicosis is excess circulating thyroid hormone. Causes include Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma, thyroiditis (subacute, painless or postpartum), excess iodine/amiodarone, exogenous thyroid hormone, hCG-mediated disease, a TSH-secreting pituitary tumor, struma ovarii and functioning metastases. Symptoms: heat intolerance, sweating, weight loss, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, proximal weakness and increased bowel frequency. Signs: tachycardia/atrial fibrillation, warm moist skin, fine tremor, hyperreflexia and goiter; Graves may add eye signs and pretibial dermopathy. Confirm with suppressed TSH and raised free T4 and/or T3. Use TSH-receptor antibodies for Graves; uptake/scintigraphy distinguishes diffuse Graves uptake, focal toxic nodules and low-uptake thyroiditis or exogenous hormone. Treat the cause: beta blocker for symptoms; antithyroid drugs for hormone synthesis; radioactive iodine or surgery for Graves/autonomous nodules when appropriate; thyroiditis is generally treated symptomatically, not with thionamides.

### Q44: Define (Subclinical hypothyroidism)

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Subclinical hypothyroidism is a raised serum TSH with free T4 within the reference range (usually with no or mild symptoms). It may be transient or reflect early autoimmune thyroiditis, prior thyroid treatment, iodine imbalance or a drug effect. Confirm with repeat TSH/free T4 and assess thyroid peroxidase antibodies and symptoms. Consider levothyroxine when TSH is persistently at least 10 mIU/L, or at lower elevations in selected symptomatic patients, pregnancy/planning pregnancy, positive TPO antibodies, goiter or relevant cardiovascular risk. Otherwise monitor TSH and free T4 periodically. Recheck TSH about 6–8 weeks after starting or changing levothyroxine.

### Q45: Subacute thyroiditis (De Quervan thyroiditis): C/P, investigations & ttt

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Subacute (De Quervain) thyroiditis is a usually post-viral, painful inflammatory thyroiditis. It causes fever, malaise and a tender enlarged thyroid with pain that may radiate to the jaw or ear; a transient thyrotoxic phase may be followed by temporary hypothyroidism and then recovery. Early tests show suppressed TSH and raised free T4/T3; ESR/CRP are elevated and radioactive iodine uptake is low. Treat pain/inflammation with an NSAID; use corticosteroids for severe or persistent pain. A beta blocker can control thyrotoxic symptoms. Antithyroid drugs are not useful because hormone excess is from leakage, not increased synthesis. Give temporary levothyroxine if the hypothyroid phase is symptomatic or prolonged, with follow-up thyroid function tests.

### Q46: Compare between Graves' disease & toxic multinodular goiter [symptoms, signs, laboratory and radiological diagnosis]

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Graves disease is autoimmune and commonly affects younger adults; toxic multinodular goiter is more common in older people and often follows longstanding nodular goiter. Graves produces a diffuse, smooth goiter, sometimes with bruit, ophthalmopathy and pretibial dermopathy; toxic multinodular goiter has an irregular nodular gland and no characteristic Graves eye/skin disease. Both suppress TSH and raise free T4 and/or T3. TSH-receptor antibodies are usually positive in Graves and negative in toxic multinodular goiter. Radioiodine uptake is diffusely increased in Graves and patchy/heterogeneous with autonomous nodules in toxic multinodular goiter; ultrasound shows diffuse hypervascularity versus multiple nodules. Treatment options include antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine or surgery, individualized to cause, age, comorbidity, goiter size and patient preference.

### Q47: Cancer thyroid: Discuss risk factor for from the history, examination & investigation

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Thyroid cancer risk clues include childhood head/neck irradiation, a first-degree relative with thyroid cancer, familial medullary cancer or MEN2, and relevant inherited syndromes. Concerning history/examination includes a rapidly enlarging, hard or fixed nodule, hoarseness, dysphagia, compressive symptoms, cervical lymphadenopathy or vocal-cord palsy. Evaluate with TSH and high-quality thyroid/neck ultrasound, including cervical nodes; perform ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration when sonographic pattern and nodule size meet criteria. If TSH is low, radionuclide scanning identifies a functioning nodule, which is rarely malignant. Cytology guides surveillance, repeat sampling or surgery. For suspected medullary cancer, measure calcitonin and evaluate for RET/MEN2 as indicated.

### Q48: Solitary thyroid nodule: the approaches to diagnosis

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** For a solitary thyroid nodule, take a history of growth, radiation exposure, family history, hoarseness and compressive symptoms; examine the thyroid, cervical nodes and voice. Measure TSH and perform thyroid/neck ultrasound to characterize the nodule and lymph nodes. If TSH is suppressed, obtain radionuclide scintigraphy: a hyperfunctioning nodule rarely needs cytology, while nonfunctioning nodules are assessed by ultrasound risk and size. Perform ultrasound-guided FNA when indicated and manage according to cytology (Bethesda): observe benign nodules with appropriate follow-up, repeat sampling for nondiagnostic/indeterminate results, and refer suspicious or malignant cytology for surgery. Consider surgery for compression, growth, suspicious cytology or patient-specific reasons.

### Q49: hypothyroidism Discuss Causes, Clinical types, Diagnosis and management Cases questions

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 5
**EXP:** Causes: primary thyroid failure (Hashimoto thyroiditis, iodine deficiency or excess, thyroidectomy/radioiodine, congenital dysgenesis or dyshormonogenesis, and drugs such as lithium or amiodarone) and central hypothyroidism from pituitary/hypothalamic disease. Types include overt primary, subclinical, central and congenital hypothyroidism. Features include fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry coarse skin/hair, hoarse voice, bradycardia, delayed relaxation of reflexes, menstrual disturbance and nonpitting myxedema. In primary disease TSH is high and free T4 low (subclinical: TSH high, free T4 normal); in central disease free T4 is low with an inappropriately low/normal TSH. Check TPO antibodies when autoimmune thyroiditis is suspected and evaluate pituitary/adrenal function in central disease. Treat with oral levothyroxine, individualized by age, weight and cardiac status; take consistently and separate from iron/calcium. Recheck TSH in about 6–8 weeks after a dose change and adjust; follow free T4 rather than TSH in central hypothyroidism. Treat adrenal insufficiency before starting thyroid hormone when suspected.

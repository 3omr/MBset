### Q44: Why are angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors not recommended during pregnancy?

**Type:** QROC

**Model Answer:**
ACE inhibitors are strictly contraindicated in pregnancy (Pregnancy Category D / Black Box Warning) because they cross the placenta and disrupt fetal renal hemodynamics. This causes fetal renal tubular dysgenesis, oligohydramnios (due to decreased fetal urine output), pulmonary hypoplasia, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), calvarial/skull hypoplasia, and fetal death.

### Q45: Why might a healthcare provider choose an angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) over an ACE inhibitor?

**Type:** QROC

**Model Answer:**
ARBs selectively block AT1 receptors without inhibiting ACE (kininase II). Because ACE also degrades bradykinin, ACE inhibitors cause bradykinin accumulation in the airways, precipitating a persistent dry cough in 10-20% of patients and angioedema in susceptible individuals. ARBs provide equivalent hemodynamic and renoprotective benefits without increasing bradykinin, making them the preferred alternative for patients experiencing ACEi-induced cough or angioedema.

### Q46: Why is hydralazine often combined with a beta-blocker and diuretic when used to treat hypertension?

**Type:** QROC

**Model Answer:**
Hydralazine is a direct arteriolar vasodilator that decreases total peripheral resistance. This marked arterial dilation triggers two powerful compensatory reflex responses: 1) Baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic reflex activation, causing tachycardia and increased cardiac output (triggering palpitations or angina); 2) Decreased renal perfusion pressure and sympathetic activation of juxtaglomerular cells, activating RAAS and causing salt and water retention (pseudotolerance/edema). Co-administration of a beta-blocker (e.g. propranolol/metoprolol) blunts reflex tachycardia, and a diuretic (e.g. furosemide/thiazide) prevents fluid retention, creating synergistic blood pressure control.

### Q47: Why should beta-blockers be used with caution or avoided in patients with asthma or COPD?

**Type:** QROC

**Model Answer:**
Non-selective beta-blockers (such as propranolol) block beta-2 adrenergic receptors on bronchial smooth muscle cells, inhibiting physiological bronchodilation and precipitating severe, potentially life-threatening bronchospasm. Even cardioselective beta-1 blockers (e.g. metoprolol, atenolol) lose receptor selectivity at higher doses and should be used with extreme caution in asthmatic patients.

### Q48: Why might a healthcare provider prescribe an ACE inhibitor for a patient with heart failure?

**Type:** QROC

**Model Answer:**
In heart failure, neurohormonal activation of the RAAS increases systemic vascular resistance (afterload) and fluid retention (preload), promoting adverse cardiac remodeling and ventricular dilation. ACE inhibitors block angiotensin II formation: 1) Reduce afterload by arterial vasodilation, improving cardiac output; 2) Reduce preload by venodilation and decreased aldosterone secretion; 3) Inhibit neurohormonally mediated cardiac myocyte hypertrophy, apoptosis, and myocardial fibrosis, conferring proven long-term survival and mortality reduction.

### Q49: Why is it crucial to monitor serum potassium and renal function in patients taking spironolactone or eplerenone for severe heart failure?

**Type:** QROC

**Model Answer:**
Spironolactone and eplerenone are mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) that competitively block aldosterone receptors in the renal cortical collecting tubules. This inhibits Na+ reabsorption and K+/H+ excretion. When combined with ACE inhibitors or ARBs, there is a substantial risk of developing severe, life-threatening hyperkalemia (>5.5 mEq/L) and acute worsening of renal function. Routine monitoring of serum electrolytes and creatinine is essential to prevent fatal cardiac arrhythmias.

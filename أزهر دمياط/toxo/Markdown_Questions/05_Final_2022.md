## Section 2: Written Questions

### Q11: State True or False: In methanol toxicity, the anion gap is decreased.

**Model Answer:**
False. Methanol is metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase into formic acid, producing a severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (HAGMA).

---

### Q12: State True or False: Gastric lavage is an absolute contraindication in kerosene (hydrocarbon) poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
True. Gastric lavage and emesis are strictly contraindicated in hydrocarbon ingestions because induced regurgitation leads to pulmonary aspiration and fatal chemical pneumonitis.

---

### Q13: State True or False: Children should receive the same dose of antivenin as adults.

**Model Answer:**
True. Antivenin dosage depends on neutralizing the total quantity of venom injected by the snake, not on patient weight; children may even require larger doses.

---

### Q14: State True or False: Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rarely fatal syndrome.

**Model Answer:**
False. NMS is a life-threatening medical emergency with an estimated mortality rate of 10-20% if left untreated or unmanaged.

### Q15: State True or False: The second stage in acetaminophen poisoning is the hepatic stage.

**Model Answer:**
True. Stage 2 (24-48 hours post-ingestion) represents the onset of hepatic injury with rising serum transaminases (AST/ALT) and right upper quadrant tenderness.

### Q16: Enumerate the essential emergency measures in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Essential Emergency Measures in Clinical Toxicology:
1. Resuscitation and Stabilization: Support ABCs (Airway patency, Breathing/oxygenation, Circulation/blood pressure, and Disability/Dextrose).
2. Clinical Assessment: Prompt identification of the toxic syndrome (Toxidrome) and vital signs monitoring.
3. Decontamination: Prevent further poison absorption via dermal washing, ocular irrigation, or gastrointestinal decontamination (activated charcoal within 1 hour).
4. Antidote Administration: Prompt use of specific antidotes (e.g. Naloxone, Atropine, N-acetylcysteine, Flumazenil).
5. Enhanced Elimination: Use techniques such as hemodialysis, hemoperfusion, or forced alkaline diuresis when indicated.
6. Supportive Care: Continuous monitoring and symptomatic therapy in an intensive care setting.

---

### Q17: Enumerate the early complications of corrosive ingestion.

**Model Answer:**
Early complications of corrosive ingestion (< 24 to 48 hours):
1. Asphyxia and acute respiratory distress due to laryngeal edema or glottic spasm.
2. Severe hypovolemic and neurogenic shock from agonizing pain and massive fluid loss.
3. Acute gastrointestinal perforation (most commonly stomach or esophagus) leading to chemical peritonitis or mediastinitis.
4. Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) and systemic metabolic acidosis.

---

### Q18: Explain the mechanism of action of Carbon Monoxide (CO) poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of Action of Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning:
1. High Hemoglobin Affinity: CO binds hemoglobin with an affinity 210 to 250 times greater than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) and drastically reducing blood oxygen-carrying capacity.
2. Leftward Shift of Oxyhemoglobin Curve (Haldane Effect): CO alters hemoglobin conformation, locking remaining oxygen molecules and preventing oxygen release to peripheral tissues.
3. Cellular Cytochrome Inhibition: CO enters cells and binds mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV), halting oxidative phosphorylation and causing severe cellular hypoxia and lactic acidosis.
4. Myoglobin Binding: Binds cardiac and skeletal myoglobin with high affinity, leading to direct myocardial depression, arrhythmias, and rhabdomyolysis.

---

### Q19: Mention the physiological and specific antidotes for mercury toxicity.

**Model Answer:**
Antidotes for Mercury Toxicity:
1. Dimercaprol (BAL - British Anti-Lewisite): The primary parenterally administered chelator for acute elemental and inorganic mercury salt poisoning (IM deep injection).
2. DMSA (Succimer) or DMPS: Water-soluble oral chelating agents with higher therapeutic index; effective for chronic inorganic and organic (methyl) mercury.
3. D-Penicillamine: Alternative oral chelator for chronic elemental mercury exposure (contraindicated in patients allergic to penicillin).

---

### Q20: Describe the characteristic clinical features of severe ethanol coma.

**Model Answer:**
Characteristics of Severe Ethanol Coma:
1. Hypothermia with cold, pale, clammy skin and profuse diaphoresis.
2. Marked hypotension, peripheral vasodilation, and reflex tachycardia.
3. Pupillary Signs: Pupils are usually constricted but dilate upon painful cutaneous facial pinching, then slowly re-constrict (Positive McEwen's sign).
4. Respiratory Depression: Slow, shallow, or stertorous breathing with cyanosis and strong alcoholic breath odor.
5. Neurological: Generalized muscular flaccidity, bilateral loss of corneal and deep tendon reflexes (areflexia).

### Q21: Define Antidote in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Antidote:
A specific therapeutic agent administered to counteract, neutralize, prevent, or reverse the physiological, biochemical, or toxicological effects of a specific poison or toxin.

### Q22: Define Metabolic Acidosis in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Metabolic Acidosis in Clinical Toxicology:
A primary acid-base disorder characterized by an arterial blood pH < 7.35 and serum bicarbonate < 22 mEq/L, frequently presenting with an elevated anion gap (> 12 mEq/L) following ingestion of toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), salicylates, cyanide, iron, or paraldehyde.

---

### Q23: Define Addiction (Drug Dependence) in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Addiction (Drug Dependence):
A chronic, relapsing neurobehavioral condition characterized by compulsive drug seeking and taking despite adverse health, social, and legal consequences. It encompasses psychological dependence (craving) and physical dependence (tolerance and withdrawal symptoms upon cessation).

---

### Q24: Define Botulism in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Botulism:
A severe neuroparalytic disease caused by the potent neurotoxins (mainly types A, B, E) of Clostridium botulinum, which irreversibly block the presynaptic release of acetylcholine at peripheral cholinergic synapses and neuromuscular junctions, leading to bilateral cranial nerve palsies followed by descending symmetrical flaccid paralysis.

---

### Q25: Define Punctate Basophilia (Basophilic Stippling).

**Model Answer:**
Punctate Basophilia (Basophilic Stippling):
Presence of numerous fine, dark-blue granules of aggregated ribosomal RNA evenly dispersed throughout the cytoplasm of erythrocytes on blood film.
Classic diagnostic hallmark of plumbism (lead poisoning) caused by lead-induced inhibition of pyrimidine-5'-nucleotidase.

### Q27: Mention the fatal dose of kerosene in children.

**Clinical Case:** A 5-year-old boy was admitted to the emergency department with his mother, who stated that her boy accidentally drank domestic fuel.

**Model Answer:**
Fatal Dose of Kerosene in Children:
The oral fatal dose is approximately 1 to 2 mL/kg (roughly 10 to 30 mL in young children).
However, direct pulmonary aspiration of as little as 0.2 to 0.5 mL into the tracheobronchial tree can trigger fatal chemical pneumonitis and asphyxia.

### Q28: What is the mechanism of action of kerosene toxicity?

**Clinical Case:** A 5-year-old boy was admitted to the emergency department with his mother, who stated that her boy accidentally drank domestic fuel.

**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of Action of Kerosene Toxicity:
1. Pulmonary Toxicity (Major Danger): Kerosene has low viscosity, low surface tension, and high volatility. Aspiration during ingestion or vomiting spreads liquid rapidly across the alveolar capillary bed, dissolving pulmonary surfactant, causing alveolar collapse, chemical pneumonitis, and severe non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
2. Central Nervous System Depression: Large ingested or aspirated amounts absorbed into systemic circulation cross the blood-brain barrier, causing initial euphoria followed by lethargy, stupor, coma, and medullary respiratory depression.
3. Local Mucosal Irritation: Direct irritant action causes burning of oral and gastrointestinal mucous membranes.

---

### Q29: What are the possible clinical manifestations of acute kerosene toxicity?

**Clinical Case:** A 5-year-old boy was admitted to the emergency department with his mother, who stated that her boy accidentally drank domestic fuel.

**Model Answer:**
Clinical Manifestations of Kerosene Toxicity:
1. Respiratory Signs: Immediate choking, coughing, tachypnea, grunting, intercostal retractions, cyanosis, hemoptysis, and chemical pneumonitis.
2. Gastrointestinal Signs: Burning sensation in mouth and throat, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea.
3. Central Nervous System: Drowsiness, restlessness, headache, ataxia, confusion, and seizures or coma in severe toxic ingestion.
4. Characteristic Odor: Strong petroleum/kerosene odor on the child's breath, skin, and clothing.

### Q30: Outline the immediate first aid and emergency management for this child.

**Clinical Case:** A 5-year-old boy was admitted to the emergency department with his mother, who stated that her boy accidentally drank domestic fuel.

**Model Answer:**
First Aid and Emergency Management for Kerosene Ingestion:
1. Absolute Contraindications: Do NOT induce vomiting (emesis) and do NOT perform gastric lavage. Activated charcoal is ineffective and increases vomiting/aspiration risk.
2. Decontamination: Immediately remove fuel-soaked clothing and wash contaminated skin with copious soap and water to prevent chemical burns and dermal absorption.
3. Respiratory Support: Maintain clear airway, administer warm humidified supplemental oxygen, and place child in semi-sitting position.
4. Radiological Monitoring: Obtain baseline chest X-ray and repeat at 4-6 hours post-ingestion to monitor for chemical pneumonitis.
5. Supportive Therapy: Administer nebulized bronchodilators if bronchospasm develops; prophylactic antibiotics and corticosteroids are not routinely recommended.

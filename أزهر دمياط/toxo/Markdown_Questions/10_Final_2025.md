## Section 2: Written Questions

### Q10: Outline the treatment of food poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Acute Food Poisoning:
1. Rehydration & Electrolyte Replacement: The cornerstone of management; oral rehydration solution (ORS) for mild/moderate dehydration, and IV fluids (Ringer's lactate or normal saline) for severe dehydration and shock.
2. Symptomatic Therapy: Antiemetics (Ondansetron or Metoclopramide) for persistent vomiting once obstruction is ruled out; avoid antimotility agents (Loperamide) in invasive/bloody diarrhea.
3. Specific Antimicrobial Therapy: Reserved for severe invasive bacterial enteritis (e.g. Ciprofloxacin or Azithromycin for Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter); specific antitoxin (Trivalent Botulinum Antitoxin) for botulism.
4. Dietary Management: Bland diet (BRAT) once oral intake is tolerated.

---

### Q11: Outline the treatment of corrosive poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Corrosive Poisoning:
1. Immediate Dilution: Administer small amounts of water or milk (within minutes of ingestion) to dilute the chemical; absolute contraindications to emesis, blind gastric lavage, or chemical neutralizing agents (exothermic injury).
2. Airway & Resuscitation: Early endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy if laryngeal edema or stridor develops; aggressive IV fluid resuscitation for hypovolemic shock.
3. Diagnostic Endoscopy: Flexible esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) within 12 to 24 hours to grade mucosal damage (Zargar classification) and predict stricture risk.
4. Supportive Care: IV proton pump inhibitors (Omeprazole), broad-spectrum antibiotics if perforation is suspected, and analgesics for pain relief.

---

### Q12: Outline the treatment of chronic lead poisoning (plumbism).

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Chronic Lead Poisoning (Plumbism):
1. Source Elimination: Immediately remove patient from lead-contaminated industrial/domestic environment.
2. Chelation Therapy:
 - Severe Lead Encephalopathy / High Blood Lead Level (BLL > 70 ug/dL): Combined therapy with Dimercaprol (BAL) 4 mg/kg IM every 4 hours followed by CaNa2 EDTA to prevent mobilization into brain.
 - Moderate Plumbism (BLL 45-69 ug/dL): Oral Succimer (DMSA) 10 mg/kg TID for 5 days then BID for 14 days, or IV CaNa2 EDTA.
3. Nutritional Support: High-calcium and iron-rich diet to reduce gastrointestinal lead absorption and correct iron deficiency.
4. Symptomatic: Anticonvulsants for lead encephalopathy seizures, physical therapy for wrist drop / peripheral neuropathy.

---

### Q13: Outline the treatment of kerosene (hydrocarbon) poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Kerosene Poisoning:
1. Absolute Contraindications: Do NOT induce vomiting (emesis) and do NOT perform gastric lavage or administer activated charcoal (increases aspiration risk).
2. Decontamination: Immediately remove fuel-soaked clothing and wash contaminated skin with soap and water to prevent chemical burns and dermal absorption.
3. Respiratory Support: Maintain clear airway, administer warm humidified supplemental oxygen, and place child in semi-sitting position.
4. Radiological Monitoring: Obtain baseline chest X-ray and repeat at 4-6 hours post-ingestion to monitor for chemical pneumonitis.
5. Supportive Therapy: Administer nebulized bronchodilators if bronchospasm develops; prophylactic antibiotics and corticosteroids are not routinely recommended.

---

### Q14: Outline the treatment of acute acetaminophen (paracetamol) toxicity.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Acetaminophen Toxicity:
1. Gastrointestinal Decontamination: Activated charcoal (1 g/kg) if patient presents within 1 to 2 hours of ingestion.
2. Specific Antidote (N-Acetylcysteine - NAC):
 - Administer based on Rumack-Matthew nomogram serum acetaminophen concentration drawn at 4 hours post-ingestion.
 - IV Protocol: 150 mg/kg loading dose over 1 hour, followed by 50 mg/kg over 4 hours, then 100 mg/kg over 16 hours.
 - Oral Protocol: 140 mg/kg loading dose, followed by 70 mg/kg every 4 hours for 17 doses.
 - Mechanism: Restores hepatic glutathione stores and acts as a glutathione substitute to detoxify toxic NAPQI.
3. Supportive Care: Monitor transaminases (AST/ALT), prothrombin time/INR, renal function; liver transplantation evaluation for fulminant hepatic failure.

### Q15: Define Anticholinergic Syndrome (Toxidrome).

**Model Answer:**
Anticholinergic Syndrome:
A clinical toxidrome caused by blockade of central and peripheral muscarinic acetylcholine receptors by drugs such as atropine, scopolamine, antihistamines, and tricyclic antidepressants.
Classic mnemonic: 'Blind as a bat (mydriasis), Mad as a hatter (delirium), Red as a beet (flushing), Hot as a hare (hyperthermia), Dry as a bone (anhidrosis/dry mouth), Full as a flask (urinary retention)'. Specific antidote: Physostigmine.

---

### Q16: Define Gastric Lavage in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Gastric Lavage:
A gastrointestinal decontamination procedure involving the insertion of a large-bore orogastric tube into the stomach to aspirate gastric contents and repeatedly instill and remove small aliquots of fluid (saline or water).
Indicated only within 1 hour of life-threatening ingestions; strictly contraindicated in corrosive ingestion, hydrocarbons, or comatose patients with unprotected airways.

---

### Q17: Define Diagnostic Provocation Test in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Diagnostic Provocation Test:
Administration of a specific pharmacologic antagonist in a controlled setting to confirm or exclude a suspected toxicological diagnosis.
Classic example: Naloxone Challenge Test (0.4-2 mg IV) in an unconscious patient with suspected opioid overdose, where rapid reversal of coma, miosis, and hypoventilation confirms the diagnosis.

---

### Q18: Define Drug Habituation in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Drug Habituation:
A condition resulting from the repeated consumption of a drug in which there is psychological/emotional dependence (a desire, but not a compulsive need, to continue taking the drug for a sense of well-being), with little or no tendency to increase the dose (absence of tolerance), and an absence of physical dependence (no physical withdrawal syndrome upon cessation).

---

### Q19: Define the term 'Silent Killer' in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Silent Killer:
A descriptive term applied to Carbon Monoxide (CO) gas because it is completely colorless, odorless, tasteless, and non-irritating to mucous membranes, allowing it to reach lethal concentrations in domestic and industrial environments without the victim's awareness, producing progressive cerebral hypoxia, coma, and death.

### Q21: Differential diagnosis of radio-opaque ingested materials seen on abdominal X-ray (CHIPS).

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with mild vomiting and abdominal pain. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 10 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Differential diagnosis of radiopaque substances on abdominal X-ray (Mnemonic CHIPS):
- C: Chloral hydrate, Calcium salts, Carbonate
- H: Heavy metals (Iron, Lead, Mercury, Arsenic, Bismuth)
- I: Iodinated compounds
- P: Psychotropic drugs (Phenothiazines, Enteric-coated tablets)
- S: Salicylates (enteric-coated), Sustained-release tablets, Solvents (chlorinated)

### Q22: Complete the full clinical stages of acute iron poisoning.

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with mild vomiting and abdominal pain. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 10 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Clinical Picture of Acute Iron Poisoning (4 Stages):
1. Stage 1 (0.5 to 6 hours - GIT Stage): Nausea, vomiting, hematemesis, bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain; massive fluid/blood loss can cause shock.
2. Stage 2 (6 to 24 hours - Quiescent / Latent Stage): Apparent clinical improvement as iron is cleared from circulation into tissues; cellular damage continues silently.
3. Stage 3 (12 to 48 hours - Systemic Toxicity Stage): Severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, profound shock, jaundice, acute hepatic necrosis, hypoglycemia, coagulopathy, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).
4. Stage 4 (2 to 6 weeks - Late Sequelae): Gastric outlet obstruction, pyloric stenosis, or intestinal strictures due to cicatricial fibrosis and scarring.

### Q23: What are the diagnostic investigations for this case?

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with mild vomiting and abdominal pain. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 10 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Investigations:
1. Serum Iron & Total Iron Binding Capacity (TIBC) at 4-6 hours post-ingestion (toxic level > 350-500 mcg/dL; lethal > 1000 mcg/dL).
2. Plain Abdominal X-ray: To detect radiopaque iron tablets in the stomach and track clearance.
3. Arterial Blood Gases (ABG): High anion gap metabolic acidosis.
4. Complete Blood Count (CBC): Leukocytosis (> 15,000/uL), hyperglycemia (> 150 mg/dL).
5. Liver function tests, renal panel, and coagulation profile (PT/INR).

### Q24: How to treat this case of acute iron overdose?

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with mild vomiting and abdominal pain. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 10 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment:
1. Decontamination: Whole Bowel Irrigation (WBI) with polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution (PEG-ELS) 500 mL/hr in children until rectal effluent is clear. Activated charcoal does NOT bind iron.
2. Specific Antidote (Deferoxamine / Desferal):
 - Indicated for systemic toxicity (shock, acidosis) or serum iron > 350-500 ug/dL.
 - IV infusion at 15 mg/kg/hour (max 6-8 g/day); binds free ferric iron to form water-soluble ferrioxamine excreted in urine ('vin rosé' urine).
3. Supportive Therapy: Aggressive IV crystalloid resuscitation for hypovolemia, correction of acidosis with bicarbonate, and blood transfusions for GI hemorrhage.

### Q25: What is the most common mode of iron poisoning in children and why?

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with mild vomiting and abdominal pain. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 10 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Most common mode of poisoning:
Accidental poisoning.
Reasons:
1. Prenatal and multivitamin iron preparations are widely present and easily accessible in households.
2. The tablets are typically brightly colored (red, green, brown) and sugar-coated, resembling candy or sweets to toddlers.
3. Lack of child-resistant packaging or leaving bottles within reach of curious young children.

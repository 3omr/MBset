## Section 2: Written Questions

### Q9: Enumerate the basic principles in the treatment of opioid addiction.

**Model Answer:**
Basic principles in treatment of opioid addiction:
1. Detoxification: Gradual substitution and tapering using long-acting opioid agonists (Methadone) or partial agonists (Buprenorphine), supplemented with alpha-2 agonists (Clonidine) for autonomic withdrawal symptoms.
2. Maintenance Therapy: Long-term replacement therapy with Methadone or Buprenorphine/Naloxone (Suboxone) to suppress craving and prevent illicit drug use.
3. Relapse Prevention / Antagonist Therapy: Naltrexone (oral or depot injection) after full detoxification to block opioid receptors.
4. Psychological & Social Rehabilitation: Psychotherapy, behavioral modification, cognitive therapy, and community support groups (Narcotics Anonymous).

---

### Q10: Outline the treatment of acute iron toxicity.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Acute Iron Toxicity:
1. Decontamination: Whole bowel irrigation (WBI) with polyethylene glycol solution until rectal effluent is clear. Activated charcoal does NOT adsorb iron.
2. Specific Chelation (Deferoxamine / Desferal): Continuous IV infusion at 15 mg/kg/hour (max 6-8 g/day) indicated for systemic symptoms (shock, acidosis) or serum iron > 350-500 ug/dL; binds free iron to form excretable ferrioxamine ('vin rosé' urine).
3. Resuscitation & Supportive: Aggressive IV crystalloids to correct hypovolemic shock, IV sodium bicarbonate for metabolic acidosis, and blood transfusion for severe GI bleeding.

---

### Q11: Outline the emergency treatment of acute Carbon Monoxide (CO) poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment of Acute Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning:
1. Immediate Evacuation: Remove victim from contaminated environment immediately into fresh air and maintain patent airway.
2. 100% Normobaric Oxygen: Administer high-flow 100% O2 via tight-fitting non-rebreather mask (reduces COHb half-life from 320 minutes to 80 minutes).
3. Hyperbaric Oxygen (HBO): 100% O2 at 2.5-3.0 ATA (reduces COHb half-life to 20 minutes); indicated if COHb > 25% (or > 15% in pregnancy), coma, seizures, syncope, or abnormal ECG/myocardial ischemia.
4. Supportive Therapy: Correct metabolic acidosis, treat cerebral edema with head elevation and mannitol, and monitor cardiac rhythm continuously.

---

### Q12: Outline the treatment protocol for corrosive substance ingestion.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment Protocol for Corrosive Ingestion:
1. Immediate Dilution: Administer small amounts of water or milk within minutes of ingestion; do NOT give emetics, do NOT perform blind gastric lavage, and do NOT use chemical neutralizing agents.
2. Airway Management: Early endotracheal intubation or emergency cricothyroidotomy/tracheostomy if laryngeal edema or stridor develops.
3. Fluid Resuscitation: Aggressive IV fluids to combat severe hypovolemic shock resulting from fluid sequestration in injured tissues.
4. Diagnostic Endoscopy: Perform flexible upper endoscopy within 12-24 hours to assess depth and extent of mucosal necrosis.
5. Supportive Care: IV proton pump inhibitors, analgesics for excruciating pain, and IV antibiotics if transmural perforation is suspected.

### Q13: Enumerate the clinical indications for the administration of activated charcoal.

**Model Answer:**
Indications for Activated Charcoal:
1. Ingestion of toxic amounts of adsorbable drugs or poisons within 1 to 2 hours of ingestion.
2. Ingestion of sustained-release, enteric-coated, or delayed-release pharmaceuticals.
3. Multiple-Dose Activated Charcoal (MDAC / Gastrointestinal Dialysis): To enhance elimination of drugs undergoing enterohepatic or enterovascular recirculation (e.g. Carbamazepine, Dapsone, Phenobarbital, Quinine, Theophylline).
4. As an adjunct following gastric lavage for life-threatening drug overdoses.

### Q14: Define Drug Tolerance in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Tolerance:
A state of decreased responsiveness to a pharmacological agent resulting from its prior repeated administration, requiring increasingly larger doses of the drug to produce the same biological effect originally achieved with smaller doses.
Subtypes include Pharmacokinetic (metabolic) tolerance, Pharmacodynamic (cellular/receptor downregulation) tolerance, and Cross-tolerance.

---

### Q15: Define Cyanide Kit in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Cyanide Kit (Lilly / Taylor Cyanide Antidote Package):
A specific combination of three antidotal drugs administered sequentially to treat acute cyanide poisoning:
1. Amyl Nitrite Inhalant: Rapidly inhaled to produce initial methemoglobinemia (~5%).
2. Sodium Nitrite (3% IV): Converts hemoglobin into methemoglobin (~20-30%), which binds cyanide ions with high affinity to form non-toxic cyanmethemoglobin, freeing cytochrome oxidase.
3. Sodium Thiosulfate (25% IV): Acts as a sulfur donor for the hepatic enzyme rhodanese to convert cyanide into water-soluble, nontoxic thiocyanate excreted in urine.

---

### Q16: Define Diagnostic Provocation Test in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Diagnostic Provocation Test:
Administration of a specific pharmacologic antagonist in a controlled setting to confirm or exclude a suspected toxicological diagnosis.
Classic example: Naloxone Challenge Test (0.4-2 mg IV) in an unconscious patient with suspected opioid overdose, where rapid reversal of coma, miosis, and hypoventilation confirms the diagnosis.

---

### Q17: Define Coma Cocktail in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Coma Cocktail:
A standardized empiric intravenous regimen historically administered to comatose patients with altered mental status of unknown etiology in emergency practice.
Components:
1. High-concentration Dextrose (50 mL of D50W): To diagnose and reverse acute hypoglycemia.
2. Thiamine (100 mg IV): Given before or with glucose to prevent acute Wernicke's encephalopathy in malnourished/alcoholic patients.
3. Naloxone (0.4-2.0 mg IV): Pure opioid antagonist to reverse life-threatening opioid-induced respiratory depression and coma.
4. Supplemental High-Flow Oxygen: To correct severe systemic hypoxemia.

---

### Q18: Define Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS):
A standardized neurological scoring system used to objectively assess and monitor the level of consciousness and depth of coma in acute poisoning and head trauma.
It scores three behavioral responses:
1. Eye Opening Response (1 to 4 points)
2. Best Verbal Response (1 to 5 points)
3. Best Motor Response (1 to 6 points)
Total score ranges from 3 (deep coma / brain death) to 15 (fully alert and oriented); a score <= 8 indicates severe coma necessitating immediate endotracheal intubation.

### Q20: Explain the mechanism of action of acute phenol poisoning.

**Clinical Case:** A child 2 years old was brought to the emergency department after drinking a disinfectant liquid. On examination, he was in coma, pupils are constricted, pulse was 140/min, respiratory rate was 40/min. Urine was scanty and its color changed to green on exposure to air.

**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of Action:
1. Local Coagulative Action: Precipitates cellular proteins causing chemical burns with gray-white eschars and local sensory nerve destruction (painless necrosis).
2. Systemic Action: Readily absorbed into blood, causing initial transient CNS stimulation followed by profound CNS and medullary depression, coma, hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and cardiovascular collapse.

### Q21: Complete the full clinical picture of acute phenol poisoning.

**Clinical Case:** A child 2 years old was brought to the emergency department after drinking a disinfectant liquid. On examination, he was in coma, pupils are constricted, pulse was 140/min, respiratory rate was 40/min. Urine was scanty and its color changed to green on exposure to air.

**Model Answer:**
Clinical Picture:
1. Local: Grayish-white corrosion of lips and oral mucosa, painless chemical burns, drooling, vomiting.
2. Systemic CNS: Transient excitement followed rapidly by deep coma, hyporeflexia, and respiratory depression.
3. Cardiovascular: Tachycardia, severe hypotension, and peripheral vascular collapse.
4. Respiratory: Tachypnea, pulmonary edema, and respiratory arrest.
5. Renal: Oliguria, proteinuria, hematuria, acute tubular necrosis, and characteristic Carboluria (green urine).

### Q22: Explain why the child's urine turns green on exposure to air in carbolic acid poisoning.

**Clinical Case:** A child 2 years old was brought to the emergency department after drinking a disinfectant liquid. On examination, he was in coma, pupils are constricted, pulse was 140/min, respiratory rate was 40/min. Urine was scanty and its color changed to green on exposure to air.

**Model Answer:**
Pathophysiology of Carboluria (Green Urine):
Phenol is metabolized in the liver by oxidation into hydroquinone and pyrocatechol, which are excreted in urine.
Upon exposure of the urine to atmospheric oxygen, these polyphenolic compounds undergo oxidation into dark green or olive-black colored quinone pigments.

### Q23: Differential diagnosis of poisoning that causes pin point pupils (miosis).

**Clinical Case:** A child 2 years old was brought to the emergency department after drinking a disinfectant liquid. On examination, he was in coma, pupils are constricted, pulse was 140/min, respiratory rate was 40/min. Urine was scanty and its color changed to green on exposure to air.

**Model Answer:**
Differential diagnosis of poisoning causing pinpoint pupils (miosis):
1. Opioids (Morphine, Heroin, Methadone, Fentanyl).
2. Organophosphates and Carbamates (Cholinergic toxicity).
3. Phenol / Carbolic acid (early coma stage).
4. Central alpha-2 agonists (Clonidine).
5. Pontine hemorrhage (non-toxicological differential).

### Q24: How to treat this child with acute phenol ingestion?

**Clinical Case:** A child 2 years old was brought to the emergency department after drinking a disinfectant liquid. On examination, he was in coma, pupils are constricted, pulse was 140/min, respiratory rate was 40/min. Urine was scanty and its color changed to green on exposure to air.

**Model Answer:**
Treatment Protocol for Phenol Ingestion:
1. Emergency Decontamination: Gentle gastric lavage with warm water or olive oil / castor oil (oil dissolves phenol and retards its absorption). Activated charcoal should be instilled.
2. Airway & Resuscitation: Endotracheal intubation for airway protection, mechanical ventilation, and IV fluid resuscitation.
3. Enhance Elimination: Forced alkaline diuresis with IV sodium bicarbonate to promote ionization and renal clearance.
4. Supportive: Treat metabolic acidosis, manage seizures with IV diazepam, and protect against acute tubular necrosis.

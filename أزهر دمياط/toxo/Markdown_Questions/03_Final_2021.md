## Section 2: Written Questions

### Q9: State True or False: Ethanol is a specific physiological antidote for methanol toxicity.

**Model Answer:**
True. Ethanol competitively inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase with 20x higher affinity, blocking methanol conversion to toxic formic acid.

---

### Q10: State True or False: Domestic coal gas is a mixture of coal gas & water gas.

**Model Answer:**
True. Domestic manufactured coal gas historically contains a mixture of coal gas, water gas, and producer gas rich in toxic carbon monoxide.

---

### Q11: State True or False: True cholinesterase is present in serum and liver.

**Model Answer:**
False. True (acetyl)cholinesterase is present in erythrocytes (RBCs), brain, synapses, and neuromuscular junctions. Pseudocholinesterase is in serum and liver.

### Q12: State True or False: Local signs are most marked in Cobra venom.

**Model Answer:**
False. Local signs (necrosis, edema, swelling) are minimal in Cobra (Elapid) envenomation and most marked in Viper (Viperidae) bites.

### Q13: Enumerate circumstances raising suspicion of a toxicological case in emergency practice.

**Model Answer:**
Circumstances raising suspicion of poisoning:
1. Sudden, unexplained acute illness, collapse, or coma in a previously healthy young adult or child.
2. Multiple individuals presenting simultaneously with identical acute symptoms after a shared meal, gathering, or workplace exposure.
3. Bizarre, multisystem clinical presentation that does not conform to standard physiological disease patterns.
4. Patient with a known psychiatric history, previous suicide attempts, or found alongside empty medicine bottles / suicide note.

---

### Q14: Enumerate the causes of death in corrosive poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Causes of Death in Corrosive Poisoning:
1. Early (< 24-48 hours): Asphyxia from acute laryngeal/glottic edema; severe neurogenic and hypovolemic shock; acute gastrointestinal perforation leading to mediastinitis/peritonitis; DIC.
2. Late (weeks to months): Progressive esophageal/pyloric cicatricial strictures leading to starvation and malnutrition; aspiration pneumonia; chronic renal failure; squamous cell carcinoma of the esophagus.

---

### Q15: Explain the mechanism of action of Carbon Monoxide (CO) poisoning.

**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of Action of Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning:
1. High Hemoglobin Affinity: CO binds hemoglobin with an affinity 210 to 250 times greater than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) and drastically reducing blood oxygen-carrying capacity.
2. Leftward Shift of Oxyhemoglobin Curve (Haldane Effect): CO alters hemoglobin conformation, locking remaining oxygen molecules and preventing oxygen release to peripheral tissues.
3. Cellular Cytochrome Inhibition: CO enters cells and binds mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV), halting oxidative phosphorylation and causing severe cellular hypoxia and lactic acidosis.
4. Myoglobin Binding: Binds cardiac and skeletal myoglobin with high affinity, leading to direct myocardial depression, arrhythmias, and rhabdomyolysis.

---

### Q16: Enumerate the specific opiate antidotes.

**Model Answer:**
Specific Opiate Antidotes:
1. Naloxone (Narcan): Rapid-acting, short-duration pure mu-opioid competitive antagonist (IV, IM, SC, IN); drug of choice for acute respiratory depression.
2. Nalmefene (Revex): Longer-acting pure opioid antagonist with duration matching that of most clinical opioids (4-8 hours).
3. Naltrexone (ReVia / Vivitrol): Long-acting oral and depot injectable opioid antagonist used primarily for relapse prevention and maintenance in detoxified addicts.

### Q17: Define Gastric Lavage in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Gastric Lavage:
A gastrointestinal decontamination procedure involving the insertion of a large-bore orogastric tube into the stomach to aspirate gastric contents and repeatedly instill and remove small aliquots of fluid (saline or water).
Indicated only within 1 hour of life-threatening ingestions; strictly contraindicated in corrosive ingestion, hydrocarbons, or comatose patients with unprotected airways.

---

### Q18: Define Metabolic Acidosis in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Metabolic Acidosis in Clinical Toxicology:
A primary acid-base disorder characterized by an arterial blood pH < 7.35 and serum bicarbonate < 22 mEq/L, frequently presenting with an elevated anion gap (> 12 mEq/L) following ingestion of toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), salicylates, cyanide, iron, or paraldehyde.

---

### Q19: Define Cyanide Kit in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Cyanide Kit (Lilly / Taylor Cyanide Antidote Package):
A specific combination of three antidotal drugs administered sequentially to treat acute cyanide poisoning:
1. Amyl Nitrite Inhalant: Rapidly inhaled to produce initial methemoglobinemia (~5%).
2. Sodium Nitrite (3% IV): Converts hemoglobin into methemoglobin (~20-30%), which binds cyanide ions with high affinity to form non-toxic cyanmethemoglobin, freeing cytochrome oxidase.
3. Sodium Thiosulfate (25% IV): Acts as a sulfur donor for the hepatic enzyme rhodanese to convert cyanide into water-soluble, nontoxic thiocyanate excreted in urine.

---

### Q20: Define Botulism in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Botulism:
A severe neuroparalytic disease caused by the potent neurotoxins (mainly types A, B, E) of Clostridium botulinum, which irreversibly block the presynaptic release of acetylcholine at peripheral cholinergic synapses and neuromuscular junctions, leading to bilateral cranial nerve palsies followed by descending symmetrical flaccid paralysis.

---

### Q21: Define Punctate Basophilia (Basophilic Stippling).

**Model Answer:**
Punctate Basophilia (Basophilic Stippling):
Presence of numerous fine, dark-blue granules of aggregated ribosomal RNA evenly dispersed throughout the cytoplasm of erythrocytes on blood film.
Classic diagnostic hallmark of plumbism (lead poisoning) caused by lead-induced inhibition of pyrimidine-5'-nucleotidase.

---

### Q22: Define Acrodynia (Pink Disease) in toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Acrodynia (Pink Disease):
A syndrome of chronic elemental or inorganic mercury poisoning occurring primarily in infants and young children, historically linked to teething powders containing calomel (mercurous chloride).
Characterized by painful, bright pink, desquamating, erythematous swelling of hands and feet, photophobia, profuse diaphoresis, irritability, hypotonia, and tachycardia.

---

### Q23: Define McEwen's Sign in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
McEwen's Sign:
A physical examination sign observed in patients in deep alcoholic coma. The pupils are initially constricted and sluggish, but upon application of a painful cutaneous stimulus (e.g. pinching the skin of the face or neck), the pupils promptly dilate, and then slowly return to their constricted state.

---

### Q24: Define Addiction (Drug Dependence) in clinical toxicology.

**Model Answer:**
Addiction (Drug Dependence):
A chronic, relapsing neurobehavioral condition characterized by compulsive drug seeking and taking despite adverse health, social, and legal consequences. It encompasses psychological dependence (craving) and physical dependence (tolerance and withdrawal symptoms upon cessation).

### Q26: Complete the full clinical picture of acute iron poisoning.

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with vomiting, abdominal pain and drowsiness. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 20 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Clinical Picture of Acute Iron Poisoning (4 Stages):
1. Stage 1 (0.5-6 hours - Gastrointestinal Stage): Abdominal pain, vomiting, hematemesis, melena, diarrhea, and hypovolemic shock.
2. Stage 2 (6-24 hours - Latent / Relative Stability Stage): Transient clinical improvement; iron continues intracellular uptake.
3. Stage 3 (12-48 hours - Systemic Toxicity Stage): Profound shock, severe metabolic acidosis, jaundice, acute hepatic necrosis, hypoglycemia, and multiorgan failure.
4. Stage 4 (2-6 weeks - Delayed Sequelae): Gastric cicatrization, pyloric stenosis, and bowel strictures.

---

### Q27: What are the investigations needed to prove your diagnosis in acute iron toxicity?

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with vomiting, abdominal pain and drowsiness. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 20 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Investigations Needed:
1. Serum Iron and TIBC at 4 to 6 hours post-ingestion: Serum iron > 350 ug/dL indicates toxicity; > 500 ug/dL indicates severe systemic poisoning.
2. Plain Abdominal Radiography (KUB): Visualizes radiopaque intact iron pills in the stomach or small bowel.
3. Arterial Blood Gases (ABG): Shows severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.
4. Complete Blood Count (CBC): Marked leukocytosis (> 15,000/uL), hyperglycemia (> 150 mg/dL).
5. Coagulation profile (PT/INR) and Liver function tests (AST/ALT, Bilirubin).

---

### Q28: What are the different lines of treatment for acute iron toxicity?

**Clinical Case:** A child 4 years old came to a toxicological unit with vomiting, abdominal pain and drowsiness. Her pregnant mother stated that the child ingested 20 tablets of her tonic pills 4 hours ago. Abdominal X-ray showed a radio-opacity in the stomach.

**Model Answer:**
Lines of Treatment:
1. Decontamination: Whole Bowel Irrigation (WBI) with polyethylene glycol (PEG-ELS) via nasogastric tube at 500 mL/hr in children until rectal effluent is clear. Activated charcoal is ineffective.
2. Specific Antidote (Deferoxamine / Desferal): Intravenous infusion at 15 mg/kg/hour (max 6-8 g/day); complexes free ferric iron to form excretable ferrioxamine ('vin rosé' urine).
3. Resuscitation & Supportive: Aggressive IV crystalloids to correct hypovolemic shock; IV sodium bicarbonate for metabolic acidosis; fresh frozen plasma for coagulopathy.

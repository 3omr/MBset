### Q9: Define referred pain and discuss two mechanisms explaining it.
**Model Answer:**
Definition: Pain perceived at a cutaneous somatic site distant from the actual deep visceral or musculoskeletal source of tissue damage.
Mechanisms:
1. Convergence-Projection Theory: Visceral pain afferents and cutaneous somatic nociceptive afferents converge onto the same second-order projection neurons (spinothalamic tract) in the dorsal horn. Because the brain is accustomed to receiving signals from the skin, it misinterprets visceral input as originating from the somatic dermatome.
2. Facilitation Theory: Subthreshold visceral impulses do not directly activate spinothalamic neurons but produce a subliminal excitatory fringe, facilitating sensory input from normal somatic cutaneous receptors.

**Tag:** `Exams, Final 2022`

### Q10: Compare between the stretch reflex and the inverse stretch reflex.
**Model Answer:**
1. Receptor: Stretch = Muscle spindle (intrafusal fibers); Inverse stretch = Golgi tendon organ (in tendon series).
2. Afferent nerve: Stretch = Fast Ia (and II) afferent fibers; Inverse stretch = Ib afferent fibers.
3. Synapse: Stretch = Monosynaptic (direct to alpha motor neuron); Inverse stretch = Disynaptic/polysynaptic (via inhibitory interneurons).
4. Effector response: Stretch = Contraction of stretched muscle; Inverse stretch = Sudden relaxation of contracting muscle (autogenic inhibition).
5. Physiological role: Stretch = Maintenance of muscle tone and posture; Inverse stretch = Protection of muscle and tendon from avulsion/tearing.

**Tag:** `Exams, Final 2022`

### Q11: Describe the sequential histological stages of cerebral infarction.
**Model Answer:**
1. Acute Stage (12-24 hours): Red neurons (eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nuclei), loss of Nissl bodies, intercellular edema, and infiltration by neutrophils.
2. Subacute Stage (24-72 hours to 2 weeks): Neutrophils replaced by activated microglia and blood-derived macrophages (foam cells / lipid-laden macrophages) phagocytosing necrotic debris; liquefactive necrosis with tissue softening and vascular neoproliferation.
3. Chronic Stage (>2 weeks to months): Liquefied necrotic tissue is cleared leaving a fluid-filled cystic cavity (pseudocyst), bordered by proliferating reactive gemistocytic astrocytes forming a dense glial scar (astrogliosis).

**Tag:** `Exams, Final 2022`

### Q12: Compare between Upper Motor Neuron (UMN) and Lower Motor Neuron (LMN) lesions.
**Model Answer:**
1. Muscle paralysis: UMN = Spastic paralysis (hypertonia) involving groups of muscles; LMN = Flaccid paralysis (hypotonia/atonia) of individual muscles.
2. Muscle tone: UMN = Hypertonia (clasp-knife spasticity); LMN = Hypotonia or atonia (flaccidity).
3. Tendon reflexes: UMN = Hyperactive/exaggerated deep tendon reflexes, clonus; LMN = Depressed or completely absent reflexes (hyporeflexia/areflexia).
4. Superficial reflexes: UMN = Lost (abdominal, cremasteric); LMN = Lost if reflex arc is involved.
5. Pathological reflexes: UMN = Babinski sign present; LMN = Absent.
6. Muscle wasting: UMN = Minimal (disuse atrophy only); LMN = Severe, rapid, denervation atrophy.
7. Fasciculations: UMN = Absent; LMN = Characteristic fasciculations and fibrillations present.

**Tag:** `Exams, Final 2022`

### Q13: Compare between Sabin (OPV) and Salk (IPV) poliomyelitis vaccines.
**Model Answer:**
1. Type: Sabin = Live attenuated virus; Salk = Formalin-inactivated (killed) virus.
2. Route: Sabin = Oral drops; Salk = Intramuscular or subcutaneous injection.
3. Immune response: Sabin = Induces systemic IgG and local secretory intestinal IgA (mucosal immunity); Salk = Induces systemic serum IgG only, without mucosal IgA.
4. Prevention of transmission: Sabin = Blocks gut replication and prevents shed/transmission (provides strong herd immunity); Salk = Prevents paralytic CNS disease but allows wild virus intestinal shedding.
5. Risk: Sabin = Rare reversion to neurovirulence causing vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP); Salk = Zero risk of VAPP, safe in immunocompromised.

**Tag:** `Exams, Final 2022`

### Q15: Explain why it is challenging to produce botulinum toxoid compared to tetanus toxoid.
**Model Answer:**
1. Lethal Potency & Biosafety: Botulinum toxin is the most poisonous biological substance known (LD50 ~1 ng/kg), imposing stringent biosafety level 3/4 containment during mass cultivation.
2. Serotypic Diversity: Clostridium botulinum elaborates 7 antigenically distinct toxins (A through G), necessitating a polyvalent toxoid, whereas Clostridium tetani produces a single uniform neurotoxin (tetanospasmin).
3. Antigenic Inactivation: Chemical formaldehyde detoxification easily destroys the heat-labile protective neutralizing epitopes of botulinum toxin, yielding low immunogenicity, unlike the highly stable tetanus toxoid.

### Q16: Describe the cardinal motor features of Parkinson's disease and the mechanism of levodopa therapy.
**Model Answer:**
Cardinal Features (TRAP):
1. Tremor: Resting pill-rolling tremor (4-6 Hz), worse at rest and damped during voluntary movement.
2. Rigidity: Lead-pipe or cogwheel rigidity affecting flexors and extensors equally.
3. Akinesia / Bradykinesia: Slowness in initiating and executing movements, masked facies (hypomimia), reduced arm swing.
4. Postural instability: Stooped posture, shuffling festinating gait, and impaired postural reflexes.
Mechanism of Levodopa:
Levodopa is the metabolic precursor of dopamine that crosses the blood-brain barrier via the large neutral amino acid transporter (LAT1). In the striatum, it is decarboxylated by L-aromatic amino acid decarboxylase (dopa decarboxylase) into dopamine within surviving dopaminergic terminals, restoring dopamine transmission in the striatum.

### Q17: Describe the clinical features and pharmacological management of Trigeminal Neuralgia (tic douloureux).
**Model Answer:**
Clinical Features: Sudden, excruciating, unilateral, brief paroxysmal lancinating / electric shock-like facial pain confined to the distribution of one or more divisions of the trigeminal nerve (most commonly V2 or V3), triggered by light touch, chewing, shaving, or wind on trigger zones.
Pharmacological Management:
First-line: Carbamazepine (or Oxcarbazepine), a voltage-gated sodium channel blocker that reduces high-frequency repetitive neuronal firing.
Alternative/add-on: Baclofen (GABA-B agonist), Gabapentin, or surgical microvascular decompression of aberrant cerebellar artery compressing the nerve root.

### Q18: Describe the production, circulation, and absorption pathways of Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF).
**Model Answer:**
Production: Produced at ~500 mL/day primarily by choroid plexuses in lateral, third, and fourth ventricles via active ependymal secretion and capillary ultrafiltration.
Circulation:
Lateral ventricles -> Interventricular foramina of Monro -> Third ventricle -> Cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius -> Fourth ventricle -> Foramina of Luschka (lateral) and Magendie (median) -> Subarachnoid space and basal cisterns -> Circulates upward around cerebral hemispheres and downward around spinal cord.

### Q19: Describe the course and decussation of the corticospinal (pyramidal) tract.
**Model Answer:**
Origin: Betz cells and pyramidal neurons of Brodmann area 4 (30%), area 6 (30%), and primary sensory areas 3, 1, 2 (40%).
Course: Corona radiata -> Posterior limb of internal capsule -> Middle 3/5 of crus cerebri in ventral midbrain -> Basilar pons (longitudinal fascicles) -> Medullary pyramids.
Decussation: At lower border of medulla, 85-90% of fibers decussate in the pyramidal decussation to form the Lateral Corticospinal Tract in the lateral white column of the spinal cord; remaining 10-15% uncrossed fibers form the Anterior Corticospinal Tract, which crosses at the segmental level.

### Q20: Describe the sequence of histological events during Wallerian degeneration of a peripheral nerve.
**Model Answer:**
1. Distal Axon and Myelin Disintegration: Within 24-48 hours after transection, axonal neurofilaments fragment, followed by collapse and fragmentation of the myelin sheath into ovoids and lipid droplets.
2. Phagocytosis: Schwann cells divide and along with recruited blood macrophages phagocytose axonal and myelin debris (completed within 1-2 weeks).
3. Formation of Bands of Büngner: Proliferating Schwann cells align longitudinally within intact endoneurial basal lamina tubes to form cellular conduits (Bands of Büngner) that secrete neurotrophic factors (NGF) and guide regenerating axonal sprouts (growing at 1-3 mm/day).
4. Retrograde Chromatolysis: In the cell body, eccentric nucleus migration, Nissl substance dispersion (chromatolysis), and cellular swelling reflect upregulated protein synthesis for regeneration.

### Q22: Describe the course, synaptic relays, and termination of the Lateral Spinothalamic Tract.
**Model Answer:**
1. First-Order Neurons: Pseudounipolar neurons in dorsal root ganglion carrying pain and temperature (A-delta and C fibers); enter spinal cord via dorsolateral tract of Lissauer and synapse on neurons in Rexed laminae I, II (substantia gelatinosa), and V of dorsal horn.
2. Second-Order Neurons: Dorsal horn projection neurons whose axons cross obliquely through the anterior white commissure to the opposite lateral white column, ascending as the lateral spinothalamic tract (spinal lemniscus) through medulla, pons, and midbrain.
3. Third-Order Neurons: Synapse in the Ventral Posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of thalamus, which projects through the posterior limb of internal capsule to the primary somatosensory cortex (Brodmann areas 3, 1, 2) in the postcentral gyrus.

### Q24: Describe the clinical features and anatomical basis of Syringomyelia.
**Model Answer:**
Anatomical Basis: Cavitation / syrinx formation within the central canal of the cervical spinal cord, which progressively expands and destroys the decussating spinothalamic fibers in the anterior white commissure.
Clinical Features:
1. Dissociated Sensory Loss: Bilateral loss of pain and temperature sensations with preservation of light touch, vibration, and proprioception (dorsal columns spared).
2. Segmental 'Cape-like' Distribution: Sensory deficit spans the shoulders, upper chest, and upper extremities.
3. Lower Motor Neuron signs in upper extremities (wasting of small hand muscles) from ventral horn anterior expansion.
4. Upper Motor Neuron signs in lower extremities from late corticospinal compression.

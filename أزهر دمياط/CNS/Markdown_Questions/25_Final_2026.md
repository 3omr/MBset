### Q12: Describe the mechanism of primary hyperalgesia.
**Model Answer:**
Primary hyperalgesia is increased sensitivity and lower pain threshold occurring directly within the site of tissue injury. Mechanism:
1. Peripheral sensitization of primary afferent nociceptors (A-delta and C fibers).
2. Damaged tissues and inflammatory cells release an 'inflammatory soup' containing Bradykinin, Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), Histamine, Serotonin (5-HT), Potassium ions (K+), ATP, Protons (H+), and Nerve Growth Factor (NGF).
3. These mediators bind to specific receptors and activate intracellular kinase cascades (PKA and PKC).
4. Kinases phosphorylate transducer ion channels (TRPV1 and Nav1.8 voltage-gated Na+ channels), lowering activation threshold, increasing firing rate, and converting silent nociceptors into active ones.

### Q13: Describe the mechanism of habituation.
**Model Answer:**
Habituation is a form of non-associative learning in which the response to a repeated, benign, non-reinforced stimulus progressively diminishes. Mechanism:
1. Presynaptic depression: Repetitive stimulation of sensory neurons leads to progressive inactivation of presynaptic voltage-gated Ca2+ channels.
2. Reduced intracellular Ca2+ influx: Action potential arrival produces progressively smaller Ca2+ entry into the presynaptic terminal.
3. Decreased neurotransmitter release: Diminished exocytosis of excitatory neurotransmitter (e.g., glutamate) into the synaptic cleft.
4. Progressively reduced amplitude of excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) in the motor neuron/interneuron, until behavioral response ceases.

**Tag:** `Exams, Final 2026`

### Q15: Compare EPSP and IPSP in terms of cause, result, and effect on action potential.
**Model Answer:**
1. Cause:
- EPSP (Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential): Binding of excitatory neurotransmitter (e.g., Glutamate, Acetylcholine) to ligand-gated receptors opening Na+ (or Ca2+) influx channels.
- IPSP (Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential): Binding of inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g., GABA, Glycine) opening ligand-gated Cl- influx channels or K+ efflux channels.
2. Result:
- EPSP: Local, graded, non-propagated depolarization of the postsynaptic membrane (membrane potential moves closer to firing threshold, e.g., -70 mV towards -60 mV).
- IPSP: Local, graded, non-propagated hyperpolarization of the postsynaptic membrane (membrane potential moves further from threshold, e.g., -70 mV towards -80 mV).
3. Effect on Action Potential:
- EPSP: Increases neuronal excitability; facilitates generation of an action potential at the axon hillock when threshold is reached.
- IPSP: Decreases neuronal excitability; inhibits action potential generation by driving membrane potential away from firing threshold.

### Q17: Differentiate between the normal (non-amyloidogenic) and abnormal (amyloidogenic) pathways of Amyloid precursor protein (APP) cleavage.
**Model Answer:**
1. Non-amyloidogenic Pathway (Normal physiologic processing):
- APP is cleaved first by alpha-secretase within the amyloid-beta peptide sequence, precluding the formation of intact toxic Abeta peptide.
- Releases soluble neuroprotective sAPP-alpha and a membrane-bound C83 fragment.
- C83 is subsequently cleaved by gamma-secretase to release non-toxic p3 peptide and AICD.
2. Amyloidogenic Pathway (Pathologic / Alzheimer's disease processing):
- APP is cleaved first by beta-secretase (BACE-1), releasing soluble sAPP-beta and leaving membrane-bound C99 fragment.
- C99 is cleaved by gamma-secretase (presenilin complex) at intramembranous sites to release neurotoxic Amyloid-beta peptides (Abeta-40 and especially aggregation-prone Abeta-42).
- Abeta-42 oligomerizes into fibrils, forming insoluble extracellular senile plaques that induce synaptic loss and neurodegeneration.

### Q31: Tetanus toxin (tetanospasmin) causes spastic paralysis and botulinum toxin causes flaccid paralysis, compare between both toxins regarding the mechanism.

### Q40: Classify neurons according to number of processes with examples.
**Model Answer:**
1. Unipolar / Pseudounipolar Neurons:
- Morphology: Have a single process emerging from the cell body that divides into two branches: a peripheral process (functioning as a receptive dendrite) and a central process entering the CNS.
- Examples: Primary sensory neurons in the Dorsal Root Ganglia (DRG) of spinal nerves and sensory ganglia of cranial nerves (Trigeminal ganglion of CN V, Geniculate ganglion of CN VII, Superior/inferior ganglia of CN IX and X).
2. Bipolar Neurons:
- Morphology: Possess two distinct processes extending from opposite poles of the soma: one axon and one dendrite.
- Examples: Bipolar cells of the retina, olfactory receptor neurons in the nasal olfactory epithelium, and sensory neurons in the spiral (cochlear) and vestibular ganglia of cranial nerve VIII.
3. Multipolar Neurons:
- Morphology: Possess one single axon and two or more (usually multiple) dendrites arising from the cell body; the most common morphological type of neuron in the human nervous system.
- Examples: Pyramidal cells of cerebral cortex, Purkinje cells of cerebellar cortex, and somatic motor neurons in the anterior gray horn of the spinal cord.
4. Anaxonic Neurons:
- Morphology: Small neurons where axons cannot be morphologically distinguished from dendrites.
- Examples: Amacrine cells and horizontal cells of the retina.

### Q43: Compare myelination in PNS and CNS.
**Model Answer:**
1. Myelin-forming Cell:
- PNS: Schwann cells (neurolemmocytes), derived embryologically from the neural crest.
- CNS: Oligodendrocytes, derived embryologically from neuroectoderm (neural tube).
2. Extent of Myelination per Cell:
- PNS: One Schwann cell myelinates a single internodal segment of only one individual axon.
- CNS: One oligodendrocyte extends multiple cytoplasmic processes that simultaneously myelinate internodes of up to 40-50 different axons.
3. Neurilemma (Sheath of Schwann):
- PNS: Present; the outermost nucleated cytoplasmic rim and plasma membrane of the Schwann cell surrounds the myelin sheath.
- CNS: Absent; oligodendrocyte cell bodies and cytoplasm remain distant from axonal internodal wraps.
4. External Basal Lamina:
- PNS: Present; an external lamina continuously envelops each Schwann cell and internode.
- CNS: Absent; CNS myelin is not invested by a basal lamina.
5. Major Protein Composition:
- PNS: Contains Protein Zero (P0), Peripheral Myelin Protein 22 (PMP-22), and Myelin Basic Protein (MBP).
- CNS: Contains Proteolipid Protein (PLP), Myelin Basic Protein (MBP), and Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein (MOG).
6. Axonal Regeneration Capacity:
- PNS: Favorable for regeneration; Schwann cells survive trauma, phagocytose debris, proliferate into Bands of Büngner, and secrete neurotrophic factors that guide regenerating axons.
- CNS: Inhibitory for regeneration; damaged oligodendrocytes and reactive astrocytes secrete inhibitory molecules (e.g., Nogo-A, oligodendrocyte myelin glycoprotein) and form dense glial scars that arrest axonal outgrowth.

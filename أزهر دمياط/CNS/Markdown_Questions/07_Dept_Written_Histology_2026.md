### Q1: Explain the light microscopic (LM) and electron microscopic (EM) features of the neuronal Perikaryon.
**Model Answer:**
1. Light Microscopy (LM):
- Size: Ranges from 4 to 140 micrometers.
- Nucleus: Large, central, spherical, euchromatic (pale-staining) with a prominent dark nucleolus ("owl-eye" appearance).
- Cytoplasm: Strongly basophilic due to abundant Nissl bodies (granules) stained with basic dyes like toluidine blue; contains neurofibrils extending into axon and dendrites.
2. Electron Microscopy (EM):
- Organelles: Prominent perinuclear Golgi complex, abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum and free ribosomes (Nissl bodies), numerous mitochondria, lysosomes, and centrosome.
- Cytoskeleton: Dense network of neurofilaments (intermediate filaments, 10 nm) forming the internal skeleton, and neurotubules (microtubules, 25 nm) mediating anterograde and retrograde axonal transport.

### Q2: Describe the histological structure and contents of neuronal Dendrites.
**Model Answer:**
1. Morphology: Multiple, tapering, highly branched extensions of the perikaryon designed to receive synaptic inputs.
2. Cytoplasmic Contents: Contain the same organelles as the perikaryon (Nissl bodies, mitochondria, neurotubules, and neurofilaments), except the Golgi apparatus is absent.
3. Dendritic Spines (Gemmules): Numerous small mushroom-shaped protrusions along dendritic branches that serve as the specialized postsynaptic sites for excitatory synaptic contacts; dynamic structures that undergo remodeling during learning and memory.

### Q3: Classify neurons according to the number of processes with histological examples.
**Model Answer:**
1. Unipolar / Pseudounipolar Neurons:
- Possess a single process that emerges from the soma and bifurcates like a "T" into a peripheral sensory process and a central axon entering the CNS.
- Examples: Primary sensory neurons in the Dorsal Root Ganglia (DRG) of spinal nerves and sensory cranial nerve ganglia (CN V, VII, IX, X).
2. Bipolar Neurons:
- Possess two distinct processes arising from opposite soma poles: one dendrite and one axon.
- Examples: Bipolar cells of the retina, olfactory receptor neurons in olfactory epithelium, and spiral/vestibular ganglia of CN VIII.
3. Multipolar Neurons:
- Possess one single axon and multiple branching dendrites; the most common neuron type in the nervous system.
- Examples: Pyramidal cells of cerebral cortex, Purkinje cells of cerebellar cortex, and anterior horn somatic motor neurons of the spinal cord.
4. Anaxonic Neurons:
- Small neurons where axons cannot be distinguished from dendrites.
- Examples: Amacrine cells of the retina.

### Q4: Differentiate histologically between Spinal Ganglia and Autonomic Ganglia.
**Model Answer:**
1. Capsule: Spinal ganglia have a thick, well-defined connective tissue capsule; Autonomic ganglia have a thin, delicate capsule.
2. Arrangement of Neuronal Cell Bodies: In spinal ganglia, neurons are arranged in compact groups/clusters at the periphery separated by bundles of nerve fibers; In autonomic ganglia, neurons are randomly scattered throughout the ganglion.
3. Neuron Type: Spinal ganglia contain pseudounipolar sensory neurons with central spherical nuclei; Autonomic ganglia contain multipolar motor neurons with eccentric nuclei.
4. Satellite Glial Cells: In spinal ganglia, satellite cells form a complete, continuous ring surrounding each neuron soma; In autonomic ganglia, satellite cells form an incomplete, irregular layer.
5. Nerve Fibers: Spinal ganglia contain mostly thick, heavily myelinated sensory fibers; Autonomic ganglia contain thin preganglionic myelinated and postganglionic unmyelinated fibers.

### Q5: Differentiate between Myelination in the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) and Central Nervous System (CNS).
**Model Answer:**
1. Myelin-forming Cell: PNS myelination is performed by Schwann cells (neurolemmocytes derived from neural crest); CNS myelination is performed by Oligodendrocytes (derived from neural tube neuroectoderm).
2. Extent of Myelination: One Schwann cell myelinates only a single internodal segment of a single axon; One oligodendrocyte extends multiple cytoplasmic processes to simultaneously myelinate internodes of up to 40-50 different axons.
3. Neurilemma (Sheath of Schwann): Present in PNS as the outer nucleated rim of Schwann cell cytoplasm and plasma membrane; Completely absent in CNS (oligodendrocyte cell bodies remain separate from myelin wraps).
4. Basal Lamina: Present around each Schwann cell internode in PNS; Absent in CNS myelin.
5. Protein Composition: PNS myelin contains Protein Zero (P0), PMP-22, and MBP; CNS myelin contains Proteolipid Protein (PLP), MBP, and MOG.
6. Regenerative Capacity: PNS allows robust axonal regeneration guided by proliferating Schwann cells and bands of Büngner; CNS regeneration is arrested by inhibitory glial scars (astrogliosis and Nogo-A).

### Q6: Write short notes on the histological types and functions of Astrocytes.
**Model Answer:**
1. Morphological Types:
- Protoplasmic Astrocytes: Predominantly located in gray matter; characterized by abundant cytoplasm, a large euchromatic nucleus, and numerous short, thick, highly branched cytoplasmic processes.
- Fibrous Astrocytes: Predominantly located in white matter; characterized by long, thin, straight, relatively unbranched cytoplasmic processes containing abundant glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) intermediate filaments.
2. Microscopic Features:
- Cytoplasmic processes expand into vascular pedicles (perivascular end-feet) that envelope capillary basement membranes and the inner surface of the pia mater (glia limitans).
3. Key Functions:
- Structural and metabolic support to neurons.
- Induction and maintenance of the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB).
- Uptake and recycling of excess extracellular potassium (K+ spatial buffering) and neurotransmitters (glutamate, GABA).
- Repair and glial scar formation (gliosis/astrogliosis) following CNS injury.

### Q7: Differentiate histologically between Microglia and Oligodendrocytes.
**Model Answer:**
1. Embryological Origin: Microglia originate from mesoderm / yolk-sac hematopoietic progenitors (mononuclear phagocyte system); Oligodendrocytes originate from neuroectoderm (neural tube).
2. Location: Microglia are evenly distributed throughout both gray and white matter; Oligodendrocytes are found in white matter (interfascicular) and gray matter (perineuronal satellite cells).
3. Morphology:
- Microglia: Smallest glial cells, dark elongated/spindle-shaped nuclei, and delicate wavy spiny branching processes in the resting state; transform into large, motile, amoeboid phagocytic macrophages (gitter cells) upon activation.
- Oligodendrocytes: Small round cells with compact, darkly stained round nuclei and a clear cytoplasmic halo ("fried-egg" appearance on paraffin sections); possess a few short slender processes.
4. Primary Function:
- Microglia: Antigen presentation, immune defense, cytokine secretion, and phagocytosis of cellular debris.
- Oligodendrocytes: Synthesis and maintenance of myelin sheaths around CNS axons.

### Q8: Differentiate between non-encapsulated and encapsulated sensory nerve endings with examples.
**Model Answer:**
1. Non-encapsulated Nerve Endings (Free/Unencapsulated):
- Structure: Terminal sensory axon branches lack a connective tissue capsule; terminal fibers may be naked or associated with specialized non-neuronal cells.
- Examples: Free nerve endings (nociceptors for pain and thermoreceptors for temperature), Merkel nerve endings (Merkel cell-neurite complexes in basal epidermis for light sustained touch), and peritrichial nerve plexuses encircling hair follicles (tactile motion).
2. Encapsulated Nerve Endings:
- Structure: Terminal sensory axon endings are enclosed within a distinct, multilayered connective tissue capsule that filters mechanical energy.
- Examples: Meissner's corpuscles (dermal papillae for fine touch and low-frequency vibration), Pacinian corpuscles (deep dermis/hypodermis for deep pressure and high-frequency vibration), Ruffini endings (dermis/joints for skin stretch), and neuromuscular spindles / Golgi tendon organs (proprioception).

### Q9: Explain the histological structure of the neuromuscular muscle spindle and its role in proprioception.
**Model Answer:**
1. Capsule: Fusiform (spindle-shaped) multilayered connective tissue capsule enclosing a fluid-filled periaxial lymph space.
2. Intrafusal Muscle Fibers (6 to 14 fibers per spindle):
- Nuclear Bag Fibers (1-2 per spindle): Dynamic and static types; central expanded bag containing a cluster of numerous nuclei; insensitive to static stretch, highly sensitive to rate of stretch.
- Nuclear Chain Fibers (4-5 per spindle): Thinner, shorter fibers with nuclei arranged in a single longitudinal row; sensitive to sustained static stretch.
3. Sensory Innervation:
- Primary (Ia) Afferents: Large myelinated fibers that terminate as annulospiral endings coiled around the equatorial regions of both bag and chain fibers (respond to onset and velocity of stretch).
- Secondary (II) Afferents: Smaller fibers terminating as "flower-spray" endings on either side of the equator mainly on chain fibers (respond to steady-state length).
4. Motor Innervation:
- Gamma motor neurons from spinal anterior horn innervate the striated contractile polar ends of intrafusal fibers, regulating receptor sensitivity and tone.
5. Proprioceptive Role: Detects changes in muscle length (stretch) and the velocity of length change, mediating the monosynaptic stretch reflex and maintaining muscle tone and posture.

### Q10: Compare histologically between Pacinian corpuscles and Meissner’s corpuscles.
**Model Answer:**
1. Shape and Size: Pacinian corpuscle is large, oval/ellipsoid, up to 1-2 mm in length (visible to the naked eye); Meissner's corpuscle is smaller, elongated/cylindrical (~80-150 micrometers).
2. Location: Pacinian corpuscles are located deep in the dermis, hypodermis, periosteum, joint capsules, and mesentery; Meissner's corpuscles are located superficially in dermal papillae of hairless (glabrous) skin (fingertips, palms, soles, lips).
3. Capsule Architecture: Pacinian corpuscle has a concentric, multilayered capsule resembling a sliced onion, formed by flattened modified Schwann cell lamellae separated by fluid; Meissner's corpuscle has a thin connective tissue capsule enclosing horizontally stacked, flattened Schwann-like supporting cells.
4. Axon Termination: Pacinian corpuscle contains a single central unbranched unmyelinated axon ending; Meissner's corpuscle contains 1-2 sensory axons that zigzag between the lamellar cells.
5. Modality and Adaptation: Pacinian detects rapid high-frequency vibration (200-300 Hz) and deep pressure (very rapidly adapting); Meissner detects dynamic light touch, flutter (30-50 Hz), and fine point discrimination (rapidly adapting).

### Q11: Write short notes on Ruffini endings and Krause end-bulbs.
**Model Answer:**
1. Ruffini Endings (Corpuscles of Ruffini):
- Structure: Elongated, spindle-shaped encapsulated receptors containing branched sensory nerve terminals intertwined among bundles of collagen fibers within a thin capsule.
- Location: Deep dermis, subcutaneous tissue, joint capsules, and periodontal ligament.
- Function: Slowly adapting type II mechanoreceptors sensitive to sustained mechanical pressure, skin stretch, and joint torque/rotation.
2. Krause End-Bulbs (Bulboid corpuscles):
- Structure: Spherical or oval encapsulated receptors containing a delicate capsule enclosing a central core of coiled, branching sensory nerve terminals.
- Location: Mucocutaneous junctions (lips, glans penis, clitoris), bulbar conjunctiva, and epineurium.
- Function: Rapidly adapting low-frequency mechanoreceptors sensitive to light touch and flutter.

### Q12: Describe the histological structure of the Meninges covering the central nervous system.
**Model Answer:**
1. Dura Mater (Pachymeninx):
- Thick, tough, dense irregular fibrous connective tissue.
- In the cranium, consists of an outer periosteal layer and an inner meningeal layer (which reflects to form falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli, enclosing dural venous sinuses).
- In the spine, separated from vertebral periosteum by the epidural space containing fat and internal vertebral venous plexus.
- Inner surface lined by flattened meningothelial cells.
2. Arachnoid Mater (Leptomeninx):
- Avascular, delicate membrane consisting of a barrier layer of tightly joined meningothelial cells and delicate, web-like fibrous trabeculae traversing the subarachnoid space.
- Subarachnoid space contains circulating CSF and cerebral blood vessels.
- Arachnoid villi and granulations (Pacchionian bodies) herniate into dural venous sinuses to reabsorb CSF.
3. Pia Mater (Leptomeninx):
- Highly vascular, delicate layer of connective tissue cells and collagen fibrils tightly adherent to the outer contour of brain and spinal cord surface, following all sulci and gyri.
- Separated from neural tissue by the glial limiting membrane (astrocyte end-feet).

### Q13: Discuss the histological differences in transverse sections of the spinal cord at cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral levels.
**Model Answer:**
1. Shape and Size: Cervical section is large and oval (flattened anteroposteriorly); Thoracic is small and circular; Lumbar is large and circular; Sacral is small and quadrilateral.
2. Gray Matter Proportions:
- Cervical: Massive gray matter with enlarged anterior gray horns (brachial plexus motor innervation to upper limbs).
- Thoracic: Relatively small amount of gray matter with slender anterior and posterior horns; prominent lateral gray horn (T1-L2 sympathetic intermediolateral column).
- Lumbar: Massive gray matter with large anterior and posterior gray horns (lumbosacral plexus to lower limbs).
- Sacral: Thick gray matter relative to white matter (thin peripheral rim of white matter); lateral gray horn present in S2-S4 (parasympathetic preganglionic neurons).
3. White Matter Volume: Highest in cervical levels (contains all ascending and descending fibers, both fasciculus gracilis and cuneatus); decreases progressively down to sacral levels (thinnest white matter rim).

### Q14: Explain the Rexed laminae architecture of the spinal cord gray matter.
**Model Answer:**
The gray matter is organized cytoarchitecturally into 10 Rexed laminae:
1. Lamina I (Posteromarginal nucleus): Caps the apex of posterior horn, receives A-delta nociceptive inputs.
2. Lamina II (Substantia Gelatinosa of Rolando): Gelatinous zone of small interneurons that modulates and gates pain/temperature transmission (Gate Control Theory).
3. Laminae III & IV (Nucleus Proprius): Main receptive zone for tactile, crude touch, and proprioceptive fibers, origin of spinothalamic tracts.
4. Lamina V & VI: Base of posterior horn; receive sensory and descending corticospinal inputs; contains Clarke's column (nucleus dorsalis, C8-L3) at base mediating dorsal spinocerebellar tract.
5. Lamina VII (Zona Intermedia): Intermediolateral sympathetic column (T1-L2) and sacral parasympathetic (S2-S4) preganglionic neurons.
6. Lamina VIII: Interneurons in anterior horn mediating reflex coordination.
7. Lamina IX: Clusters of large alpha motor neurons and gamma motor neurons innervating skeletal muscles.
8. Lamina X: Neuroglia and decussating unmyelinated axons surrounding the central canal.

### Q15: Describe the characteristic histological features of the thoracic spinal cord in transverse section.
**Model Answer:**
1. Shape: Small, circular cross-section.
2. Gray Matter: Relatively slender H-shaped gray matter. Anterior horns are small and narrow (innervating only intercostal and back muscles, no limb enlargements).
3. Lateral Gray Horn: Well-developed, distinctive triangular lateral horn extending from T1 to T12 (and L1-L2), housing the intermediolateral nucleus of preganglionic sympathetic neurons.
4. Nucleus Dorsalis (Clarke's Column): Prominent, well-demarcated rounded cell group at the medial base of posterior horn (C8-L3) giving origin to the dorsal (posterior) spinocerebellar tract.
5. White Matter: Well-developed white matter containing both fasciculus gracilis (medial) and fasciculus cuneatus (lateral) separated by the posterior intermediate septum in upper thoracic levels (T1-T6).

### Q16: Describe the ascending sensory tracts of the spinal cord and their modalities.
**Model Answer:**
1. Dorsal Column-Medial Lemniscal System (Fasciculus Gracilis & Fasciculus Cuneatus):
- Modalities: Fine discriminative touch, conscious proprioception, two-point discrimination, and vibration sense.
- Course: Ascends uncrossed in dorsal funiculus to gracile and cuneate nuclei of medulla.
2. Lateral Spinothalamic Tract:
- Modalities: Pain and thermal sensations.
- Course: Second-order fibers from posterior horn decussate in anterior white commissure and ascend in lateral funiculus to VPL thalamus.
3. Anterior (Ventral) Spinothalamic Tract:
- Modalities: Crude (light) touch and pressure.
- Course: Decussates obliquely in anterior white commissure and ascends in anterior funiculus to VPL thalamus.
4. Spinocerebellar Tracts (Dorsal and Ventral):
- Modalities: Unconscious proprioception from muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs, and joint receptors to the cerebellar cortex.

### Q18: Explain the nuclei and fiber tracts of the auditory pathway in the pons.
**Model Answer:**
1. Cochlear Nuclei: Located at the pontomedullary junction on the lateral aspect of the inferior cerebellar peduncle (dorsal and ventral cochlear nuclei receiving primary auditory fibers of CN VIII).
2. Trapezoid Body (Corpus Trapezoideum): Prominent transverse acoustic fiber decussation in the ventral tegmentum of the caudal pons formed by crossing fibers of the ventral cochlear nuclei.
3. Superior Olivary Complex: Nuclei embedded within the trapezoid body that receive bilateral auditory input, essential for auditory localization of sound in the horizontal plane.
4. Nucleus of Trapezoid Body: Relay and inhibitory interneurons within the auditory pathway.
5. Lateral Lemniscus: Prominent ascending tract formed by crossed and uncrossed acoustic fibers originating from the trapezoid body and superior olive, coursing through the lateral pontine tegmentum to terminate in the inferior colliculus of the midbrain.

### Q19: Describe the histological levels of the pons in transverse section (caudal vs. rostral).
**Model Answer:**
1. General Division: Every pontine level is divided into an anterior Basilar Pons and a posterior Tegmentum.
- Basilar Part: Contains longitudinal corticospinal and corticopontine bundles, interspersed with scattered pontine nuclei, and transverse pontocerebellar fibers entering the middle cerebellar peduncle.
2. Caudal Pontine Level (Facial Colliculus Level):
- Tegmentum contains the Abducent nucleus (CN VI) in the ventricular floor, encircled by motor fibers of the Facial nerve (CN VII) forming the internal genu (facial colliculus).
- Motor nucleus of facial nerve located ventrolaterally.
- Trapezoid body and spinal tract/nucleus of CN V present laterally.
3. Rostral Pontine Level (Trigeminal Level):
- Characterized by the emergence of the large sensory and small motor nuclei of the Trigeminal nerve (CN V) in the lateral tegmentum.
- Superior cerebellar peduncles (brachia conjunctiva) form the dorsolateral boundaries of the fourth ventricle, interconnected by the superior medullary velum.
- Medial lemniscus rotates into a horizontal ribbon at the boundary between basilar pons and tegmentum.

### Q20: Describe the cranial nerve nuclei present in the midbrain in transverse section.

### Q21: Describe the four brainstem lemnisci: origin, course, termination, and modalities.
**Model Answer:**
1. Medial Lemniscus:
- Origin: Gracile and cuneate nuclei of closed medulla (internal arcuate fibers decussating in sensory decussation).
- Modalities: Fine discriminative touch, vibration, conscious proprioception from contralateral trunk and limbs.
- Termination: Ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus.

- Origin: Second-order neurons of posterior horn (lateral and anterior spinothalamic tracts) crossing in anterior white commissure.

3. Trigeminal Lemniscus:
- Origin: Principal sensory nucleus and spinal nucleus of trigeminal nerve (CN V).
- Modalities: Somatosensory input (pain, temperature, touch) from face and head.
- Termination: Ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus of the thalamus.
4. Lateral Lemniscus:
- Origin: Trapezoid body, superior olivary nuclei, and contralateral cochlear nuclei.
- Modalities: Auditory hearing pathway.
- Termination: Inferior colliculus of the midbrain.

### Q22: Enumerate and describe the histological layers of the Cerebral Cortex.
**Model Answer:**
The neocortex (isocortex) consists of six distinct histological laminae from superficial to deep:
1. Layer I - Molecular (Plexiform) Layer: Acellular appearance, consists mostly of horizontally oriented dendrites and axons, with few scattered horizontal cells of Cajal.
2. Layer II - External Granular Layer: Densely packed with small pyramidal cells and stellate (granule) interneurons; establishes intracortical connections.
3. Layer III - External Pyramidal Layer: Composed of medium-sized pyramidal cells whose axons form association and commissural fibers (e.g., corpus callosum).
4. Layer IV - Internal Granular Layer: Densely packed stellate cells receiving the primary specific thalamocortical afferent projections; highly developed in primary sensory cortices (granular/koniocortex).
5. Layer V - Internal Pyramidal (Ganglionic) Layer: Contains large to giant pyramidal cells (Betz cells in Area 4); principal source of subcortical efferent projection fibers (corticospinal, corticobulbar, corticostriatal tracts).
6. Layer VI - Multiform (Polymorphic) Layer: Heterogeneous mixture of fusiform and Martinotti cells, projecting corticothalamic feedback fibers back to the thalamus.

### Q23: Enumerate and describe the histological layers and synaptic circuitry of the Cerebellar Cortex.
**Model Answer:**
1. Three Histological Layers (superficial to deep):
- Molecular Layer: Low cellularity; contains outer stellate cells, inner basket cells, parallel fibers (unmyelinated axons of granule cells), and the extensive dendritic arbors of Purkinje cells.
- Purkinje Cell Layer: Monolayer of huge, flask-shaped Purkinje neurons; their heavily branched dendritic trees receive excitatory climbing and parallel fibers; their axons provide the sole inhibitory (GABAergic) outflow from cerebellar cortex to deep cerebellar nuclei.
- Granular Layer: Extremely cellular; contains millions of tightly packed tiny granule cells (glutamatergic excitatory interneurons) and inhibitory Golgi type II cells, forming complex synaptic complexes called cerebellar glomeruli.
2. Afferent Input Fibers:
- Climbing Fibers: Originate exclusively from contralateral inferior olivary nucleus, wrap directly around Purkinje dendrites, firing powerful "complex spikes".
- Mossy Fibers: Originate from all other sources (vestibular, spinocerebellar, pontocerebellar), terminate in cerebellar glomeruli on granule cell dendrites, firing "simple spikes".

### Q24: Describe the histological structure and function of the Choroid Plexus.
**Model Answer:**
1. Location: Folds of vascular tela choroidea projecting into the lateral, third, and fourth brain ventricles.
2. Histological Structure:
- Core: Highly vascularized, loose connective tissue stroma derived from pia mater, containing wide, fenestrated capillaries (allowing rapid fluid exchange).
- Epithelial Lining: Single continuous layer of specialized modified cuboidal ependymal cells (choroid epithelial cells) resting on a basement membrane.
- Apical Surface: Expanded with prominent microvilli forming a brush border that increases surface area for secretion.
- Intercellular Junctions: Sealed apicolaterally by continuous, impermeable tight junctions (zonulae occludentes), which constitute the functional Blood-CSF Barrier.
3. Function: Active secretion of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) via energy-dependent ion transport (Na+/K+ ATPase pumps, carbonic anhydrase, and aquaporin-1 water channels); secretes ~500 mL of CSF daily.

### Q25: Enumerate and describe the histological components of the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB).
**Model Answer:**
The Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) is a highly selective physiological boundary composed of three essential morphological elements:
1. Non-fenestrated Capillary Endothelium:
- Continuous capillary endothelial cells lacking transcellular fenestrations or pinocytotic vesicles.
- Joined together by continuous, high-resistance, impermeable tight junctions (zonulae occludentes) composed of transmembrane proteins (claudin-3, claudin-5, occludin) linked to the cytoskeleton via zonula occludens (ZO-1, ZO-2) scaffolding proteins.
2. Continuous Capillary Basal Lamina:
- Thick, uninterrupted basement membrane enveloping the abluminal endothelial surface and embedding pericytes.
3. Perivascular Astrocytic End-Feet (Podocytes):
- Foot processes of protoplasmic and fibrous astrocytes that almost completely (approx. 95%) encircle and sheath the outer capillary wall, inducing and maintaining endothelial tight junction integrity through biochemical signaling (e.g., sonic hedgehog, angiopoietin-1).
(Pericytes also reside within the basal lamina, regulating capillary diameter and endothelial stability).

### Q26: Enumerate and describe the histological components of the Blood-CSF Barrier and contrast it with the Blood-Brain Barrier.
**Model Answer:**
1. Blood-CSF Barrier Components (in Choroid Plexus):
- Fenestrated capillary endothelium: Capillaries inside choroid plexus are fenestrated and permeable to water and solutes.
- Pial connective tissue stroma: Intervening loose basement membrane and connective tissue.
- Choroid Plexus Epithelial Cells: Simple cuboidal ependymal cells joined apically by continuous, impermeable tight junctions (zonulae occludentes). The barrier is therefore located at the choroid epithelial cell layer, NOT at the capillary endothelium.
2. Contrast with Blood-Brain Barrier:
- Site of Tight Junctions: In the BBB, tight junctions are located between capillary endothelial cells; in the Blood-CSF barrier, tight junctions are located between epithelial ependymal cells of the choroid plexus.
- Capillary Type: BBB capillaries are continuous and non-fenestrated; Choroid plexus capillaries are fenestrated.
- Astrocyte End-Feet: Essential for BBB formation; absent at the choroid plexus.

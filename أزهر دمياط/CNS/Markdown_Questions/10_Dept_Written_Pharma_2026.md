### Q1: Describe the cellular mechanism of action of Opioid receptor agonists.
**Model Answer:**
Opioids bind to Gi/Go-coupled opioid receptors (mu, kappa, delta):
1. Presynaptically: Inhibit voltage-gated calcium channels, decreasing Ca2+ influx and inhibiting release of excitatory neurotransmitters (Substance P, glutamate).
2. Postsynaptically: Open G-protein-coupled inward rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels, causing K+ efflux and membrane hyperpolarization, dampening neuronal excitability.

**Model Answer:**

2. Out of IT (Sedation and mental clouding).

7. Nausea and emesis (stimulation of CTZ).

### Q4: Mention the contraindications of Morphine and explain why.
**Model Answer:**
1. Head injury: Respiratory depression causes CO2 retention leading to cerebral vasodilation and increased intracranial pressure (risk of herniation/hemorrhage); masks pupillary signs.
2. Bronchial asthma: Respiratory depression and histamine release precipitate bronchospasm.
3. Undiagnosed acute abdomen: Analgesia masks surgical diagnostic signs.
4. Biliary colic: Spasm of the sphincter of Oddi increases intrabiliary pressure.
5. Elderly: Super-sensitivity to CNS depression and urinary retention.
6. MAO inhibitors: Severe drug interaction leading to hyperpyrexia, coma, or hypertensive crisis.

### Q5: Explain why Morphine is preferred to be given parenterally (IV/SC) rather than orally for acute severe pain.
**Model Answer:**
Because morphine undergoes extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism (approx. 75%), resulting in low and variable oral bioavailability (approx. 25%). Parenteral administration provides rapid onset and predictable plasma concentrations.

### Q10: Explain why a patient with renal failure develops severe respiratory depression and toxicity after receiving standard doses of morphine.
**Model Answer:**
Morphine is metabolized to morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G), an active metabolite with potent mu-receptor agonist activity that is cleared exclusively by the kidneys. In renal impairment, M6G accumulates and crosses the BBB, producing profound CNS and respiratory depression.

### Q11: Compare between Acute and Chronic Opioid Toxicity in terms of causes, clinical picture, and management.
**Model Answer:**
Acute Opioid Toxicity:
- Cause: Administration of large single overdose.
- Clinical picture (Classic triad): Coma, pinpoint pupils (miosis), and severe respiratory depression (shallow, slow breathing, cyanosis).
- Management: Airway protection and mechanical ventilation, gastric lavage (if ingested), and specific antidote Naloxone IV (0.4-2 mg repeated as needed).
Chronic Opioid Toxicity (Dependence/Tolerance):
- Cause: Repeated long-term administration.
- Clinical picture: High tolerance to analgesia/euphoria (no tolerance to miosis or constipation); physical and psychological dependence; severe withdrawal syndrome (rhinorrhea, lacrimation, yawning, piloerection, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, tremors) upon discontinuation.
- Management: Gradual detoxification with long-acting opioids (Methadone or Buprenorphine), psychological support, and Clonidine for autonomic symptoms.

### Q28: Describe the mechanism of action, side effects, and contraindications of Ergot alkaloids in migraine.
**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of action: Non-selective partial agonists at 5-HT1B/1D serotonin, alpha-adrenergic, and dopamine receptors. Induce vasoconstriction of dilated intracranial blood vessels and decrease release of vasodilator neuropeptides from trigeminal nerve endings.
Side effects: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, anginal chest pain, peripheral ischemia/paresthesia (ergotism), and rebound medication-overuse headache upon prolonged use.
Contraindications: Pregnancy (oxytocic action causing uterine contractions), peripheral vascular disease, ischemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, renal/hepatic impairment, and co-administration with triptans.

### Q29: Describe the mechanism of action, side effects, and contraindications of Triptans in migraine.
**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of action: Selective agonists at 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D receptors:
1. 5-HT1B stimulation causes selective vasoconstriction of painful dilated intracranial and dural vessels.
2. 5-HT1D presynaptic stimulation inhibits release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P from trigeminal sensory afferents.
3. Inhibits central transmission in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis.
Side effects: Tingling, warmth, burning sensation, dizziness, neck and chest tightness ("triptan chest sensations"), coronary vasospasm.
Contraindications (HIRP): History of ischemic heart disease / angina / prior MI, uncontrolled hypertension, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease / prior stroke or TIA, severe hepatic/renal impairment, and concurrent use with MAOIs, SSRIs, or ergotamines within 24 hours.

### Q39: Explain why Levodopa (L-Dopa) is usually combined with Carbidopa in Parkinsonism.
**Model Answer:**
Carbidopa is a peripheral dopa-decarboxylase inhibitor that does not cross the blood-brain barrier. It prevents peripheral conversion of L-Dopa to dopamine outside the brain, which:
1. Increases bioavailability and penetration of L-Dopa across the BBB into the CNS.
2. Decreases the required therapeutic dose of L-Dopa by approx. 75%.
3. Reduces peripheral dopamine-mediated side effects (nausea, vomiting, cardiac arrhythmias, orthostatic hypotension).

**Tag:** `Department, Pharmacology 2026`

### Q40: Explain why Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine) is contraindicated in patients taking Levodopa alone.
**Model Answer:**
Pyridoxine (Vit B6) acts as an essential cofactor for the enzyme dopa decarboxylase. It accelerates the peripheral decarboxylation of L-Dopa into dopamine in peripheral tissues, decreasing the amount of L-Dopa available to cross the BBB and reducing its therapeutic anti-parkinsonian effect.

### Q48: Describe the mechanism of action, side effects, and therapeutic uses of Phenytoin.
**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of action: Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels in the inactivated state, prolonging the inactivated state and preventing repetitive high-frequency neuronal firing.
Side effects:
- CNS: Nystagmus, ataxia, diplopia, drowsiness, vertigo.

Uses: 1. Focal (partial) and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. 2. Second-line agent in status epilepticus. 3. Class Ib antiarrhythmic in digitalis-induced ventricular arrhythmias.

### Q49: Explain why Phenytoin should NOT be administered by rapid IV injection or by IM injection.
**Model Answer:**
- Rapid IV injection: Phenytoin solution is strongly alkaline (pH 12) and insoluble at physiologic pH; rapid IV push causes severe local thrombophlebitis, purple glove syndrome, and catastrophic cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension, and cardiovascular collapse (must be infused < 50 mg/min).
- IM injection: Phenytoin precipitates as crystals in muscle tissue due to physiological pH, leading to erratic/delayed absorption and sterile muscle necrosis.

### Q60: Describe the clinical features and management of Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS).
**Model Answer:**
Clinical features: Hyperthermia (severe fever), severe "lead-pipe" muscle rigidity, autonomic instability (fluctuating blood pressure, tachycardia, diaphoresis), and altered mental status (delirium, stupor, coma).
Management: 1. Immediately discontinue the offending antipsychotic. 2. Intensive supportive care: aggressive cooling and hydration. 3. Pharmacological therapy: Dantrolene (skeletal muscle relaxant inhibiting calcium release from sarcoplasmic reticulum), Bromocriptine or Amantadine (dopamine agonists to overcome D2 blockade), and Diazepam for muscle spasms.

### Q61: Explain why Antidepressant drugs typically show clinical therapeutic effects only after 2 to 3 weeks of continuous treatment.
**Model Answer:**
Although neurotransmitter reuptake inhibition occurs immediately, initial increased synaptic monoamines stimulate presynaptic inhibitory autoreceptors (5-HT1A, alpha-2), which downregulates neuronal firing. Over 2 to 3 weeks of continuous therapy, these presynaptic autoreceptors become desensitized and downregulated, allowing sustained increase in postsynaptic monoaminergic neurotransmission, upregulation of secondary messenger cascades (cAMP, CREB), and induction of Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) promoting neuronal plasticity.

### Q62: Describe the mechanism of action, uses, side effects, and contraindications of Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs).
**Model Answer:**

Uses: 1. Major depressive disorder. 2. Nocturnal enuresis in children (imipramine). 3. Premature ejaculation. 4. Chronic neuropathic pain syndromes and migraine prophylaxis (amitriptyline).
Side effects (Mnemonic TCAS): Tachycardia and cardiac arrhythmias (quinidine-like cardiotoxicity), anticholinergic atropine-like effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention), Sedation and orthostatic hypotension (alpha-1 blockade), Sexual dysfunction.
Contraindications: Glaucoma, benign prostatic hyperplasia with urine retention, recent myocardial infarction / cardiac conduction blocks, epilepsy/seizures, and bipolar depression (risk of manic switch).

### Q65: Differentiate between Serotonin-Noradrenaline Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs) and Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs).

### Q70: Describe the mechanism of action and clinical uses of Benzodiazepines (BZDs).
**Model Answer:**
Mechanism of action: Bind allosterically to specific benzodiazepine (BZ) receptor sites on the GABA-A receptor complex, increasing the frequency of chloride (Cl-) channel opening in response to GABA. This increases Cl- influx, causing neuronal membrane hyperpolarization and reduced neuronal excitability (positive allosteric modulation of GABA-A).
Uses: 1. Generalized anxiety and panic disorders. 2. Insomnia (short-term). 3. Status epilepticus and acute seizures. 4. Alcohol withdrawal syndrome (prevents delirium tremens). 5. Preanesthetic medication and conscious procedural sedation.

### Q72: Describe the mechanism of action, uses, and adverse effects of Flumazenil.
**Model Answer:**
Mechanism: Competitive antagonist at the benzodiazepine binding site on the GABA-A receptor; blocks and reverses the central effects of benzodiazepines.
Uses: Reversal of benzodiazepine sedation after procedural anesthesia; diagnosis and treatment of acute benzodiazepine overdose.
Adverse effects: Nausea, projectile vomiting, precipitation of acute severe benzodiazepine withdrawal syndrome (agitation, tachycardia), and seizures (especially in patients with underlying epilepsy or TCA co-ingestion).

### Q73: Describe the mechanism of action of Barbiturates and contrast it with Benzodiazepines.
**Model Answer:**
Barbiturates bind to distinct sites on the GABA-A receptor complex and increase the duration of chloride channel opening (whereas Benzodiazepines increase the frequency of opening). At higher concentrations, barbiturates can directly open chloride channels even in the absence of GABA (GABA-mimetic action) and depress excitatory AMPA glutamate receptors, resulting in a much lower margin of safety and higher risk of fatal respiratory arrest.

### Q74: Describe the mechanism of action of Buspirone.

### Q77: Describe the mechanism of action of Local Anesthetics.
**Model Answer:**
Uncharged local anesthetic molecules diffuse across the lipid neuronal membrane into the axoplasm, where they become protonated (ionized) and bind reversibly to specific receptor sites inside the voltage-gated sodium channels. This blocks sodium influx, prevents membrane depolarization, and arrests the generation and conduction of action potentials along the nerve axon.

### Q78: Compare between Ester-linked and Amide-linked Local Anesthetics.
**Model Answer:**
Ester-linked (e.g. Cocaine, Procaine, Tetracaine):
- Duration: Short duration of action.
- Metabolism: Rapidly hydrolyzed in plasma by pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase).
- Allergic potential: Higher allergy risk due to formation of para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) metabolite.
- Stability: Less stable in solution.
Amide-linked (e.g. Lidocaine, Bupivacaine, Prilocaine):
- Duration: Longer duration of action.
- Metabolism: Degraded by hepatic microsomal cytochrome P450 enzymes.
- Allergic potential: Very low allergy risk (rare cross-reactivity).
- Stability: Highly stable.

### Q79: Why do Local Anesthetics generally have a short duration of action when injected alone?
**Model Answer:**
Because most local anesthetics possess inherent vasodilator properties. Vasodilation increases local blood flow at the injection site, accelerating the vascular absorption and systemic clearance of the anesthetic away from nerve fibers.

### Q80: How can we prolong the duration of action of Local Anesthetics and what are the benefits of adding Adrenaline (Epinephrine)?

### Q84: Outline the management of Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST).
**Model Answer:**
1. Stop local anesthetic administration immediately and call for emergency assistance.
2. Airway management: 100% oxygen, maintain ventilation, prevent hypoxia and acidosis.
3. Seizure control: Administer IV Benzodiazepines (midazolam); avoid propofol if unstable.
4. Specific antidote: Intravenous 20% Lipid Emulsion (Intralipid: bolus 1.5 mL/kg followed by infusion) to scavenge lipophilic local anesthetic from circulation.

### Q85: Enumerate the standard classes of Preanesthetic medications and explain the therapeutic purpose of each.
**Model Answer:**
1. Sedatives / Anxiolytics (Benzodiazepines: e.g. Midazolam):
 - Relieve preoperative anxiety, provide sedation, reduce CNS toxicity risk, and produce anterograde amnesia.
2. Opioids (e.g. Fentanyl, Morphine):
 - Decrease apprehension, provide preemptive analgesia, reduce the required dose of general anesthetics, and ensure smooth induction.
3. Anticholinergic drugs (e.g. Atropine, Glycopyrrolate):
 - Reduce excessive salivary, pharyngeal, and bronchial secretions, and prevent reflex vagal bradycardia and hypotension.
4. H2-receptor antagonists and Proton Pump Inhibitors (e.g. Ranitidine, Omeprazole):
 - Reduce gastric acid volume and acidity to prevent chemical Mendelson aspiration pneumonitis.
5. Antiemetics (e.g. Metoclopramide, Ondansetron):
 - Prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), enhance gastric emptying, and increase lower esophageal sphincter tone.

### Q5: Compare between Sabin (OPV) and Salk (IPV) Poliovirus vaccines.
**Model Answer:**
Sabin (OPV):
- Type: Live attenuated virus (oral drops).
- Immunity: Induces both systemic IgG and mucosal secretory IgA in gut (prevents viral shedding and provides herd immunity).
- Administration: Oral, easy, inexpensive.
- Disadvantages: Risk of Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Poliomyelitis (VAPP) and circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV); contraindicated in immunodeficiency.
Salk (IPV):
- Type: Formalin-inactivated virus (intramuscular injection).
- Immunity: Induces strong humoral IgG preventing CNS invasion, but minimal intestinal IgA (does not prevent reinfection or fecal shedding).
- Administration: Injection, requires cold chain and trained staff.
- Safety: Safe in immunocompromised individuals; zero risk of VAPP.

### Q6: Compare between the clinical features and mechanisms of Tetanus and Botulism.
**Model Answer:**
Tetanus (Clostridium tetani):
- Toxin: Tetanospasmin.
- Mechanism: Retrograde axonal transport to CNS Renshaw inhibitory interneurons, cleaving synaptobrevin and blocking release of inhibitory transmitters (Glycine and GABA).
- Clinical effect: Unopposed motor excitation causing spastic spasticity, trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus, and opisthotonos.
Botulism (Clostridium botulinum):
- Toxin: Botulinum neurotoxin (7 serotypes A-G).
- Mechanism: Acts peripherally at the neuromuscular junction, cleaving SNARE proteins to block acetylcholine release.
- Clinical effect: Symmetrical descending flaccid paralysis, starting with cranial nerves (diplopia, dysphagia, ptosis), fixed dilated pupils, and respiratory failure without sensory loss.

**Tag:** `Department, Microbiology 2026`

### Q8: Describe the methods used for laboratory diagnosis of Poliovirus infection.
**Model Answer:**
1. Viral isolation: Inoculation of cell cultures (monkey kidney, HeLa cells) from stool (feces) or throat swabs; confirmed by cytopathic effect (CPE) and neutralization by specific antisera.
2. Molecular diagnosis: Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and genomic sequencing to differentiate wild-type from vaccine-derived strains.
3. Serology: Neutralization test demonstrating fourfold rise in specific antibody titer.

### Q9: What are the main disadvantages and risks of the Sabin oral polio vaccine (OPV)?
**Model Answer:**
1. Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Poliomyelitis (VAPP): Reversion of attenuated virus to virulent phenotype during replication in gut (approx. 1 in 1-2 million doses).
2. Circulating Vaccine-Derived Poliovirus (cVDPV): In communities with low immunization coverage, prolonged transmission leads to neurovirulent strains.
3. Strict contraindication in immunocompromised patients (can cause prolonged infection and fatal paralysis).
4. Viral interference: Infection with other enteroviruses can interfere with vaccine strain replication and compromise efficacy.

### Q10: Describe the transmission and pathogenesis of Rabies virus in humans.
**Model Answer:**
Transmission: Inoculation of infected saliva via bite, scratch, or lick on broken skin/mucosa from rabid mammals (dogs, wolves, bats).
Pathogenesis: Virus replicates locally in striated muscle cells -> enters peripheral unmyelinated nerve endings via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors -> travels by retrograde fast axonal transport (50-100 mm/day) along sensory/motor axons to dorsal root ganglia and spinal cord -> spreads rapidly throughout brain causing acute encephalomyelitis with predilection for limbic system and hippocampus (forming cytoplasmic Negri bodies) -> centripetal centrifugal spread via nerves to salivary glands, cornea, and skin.

**Tag:** `Department, Microbiology 2026`

### Q12: Outline the Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) protocol for suspected Rabies exposure.
**Model Answer:**
1. Immediate wound cleansing: Copious flushing and washing with water and soap for at least 15 minutes, followed by povidone-iodine or alcohol (virucidal).
2. Rabies Immune Globulin (RIG): Infiltration of human rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG, 20 IU/kg) into and around the wound margins for passive immunization on day 0.
3. Active vaccination: Modern cell culture vaccine given on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 (with optional day 28 for immunocompromised).

### Q13: Describe the modes of transmission and prevention of West Nile virus.
**Model Answer:**
Transmission: Primary route is bite of infected Culex mosquitoes (birds are primary reservoir hosts); rare transmission via blood transfusion, organ transplantation, and transplacental spread.
Prevention: Mosquito vector control (insecticides, larvicides, eliminating standing water), personal protective measures (DEET repellents, mosquito nets, protective clothing), and blood donor screening. No licensed human vaccine exists.

### Q19: Describe the clinical specimens and diagnostic procedures used to confirm Neisseria meningitidis infection.
**Model Answer:**
Specimens: Blood and CSF (obtained via lumbar puncture with strict aseptic technique).
Procedures:
1. CSF analysis: Elevated protein, decreased glucose, maltose and glucose fermentation.
2. Direct Gram stain: Centrifuged CSF deposit shows intracellular and extracellular Gram-negative diplococci.

### Q21: Enumerate the four possible clinical outcomes of Poliovirus infection.

### Q33: Enumerate preventive measures against human Botulism.

### Q36: Outline the comprehensive management protocol for a confirmed case of Clostridium tetani infection.
**Model Answer:**
1. Passive immunotherapy: Immediate intramuscular administration of Human Tetanus Immune Globulin (HTIG, 3000-6000 units) to neutralize circulating unbound tetanospasmin.
2. Wound debridement: Surgical cleansing, debridement of necrotic tissue, and removal of foreign bodies to eradicate anaerobic focus.
3. Antibiotic therapy: Metronidazole (preferred) or Penicillin G to eliminate vegetative bacilli.
4. Supportive airway and muscle spasm control: Mechanical ventilation, dark and quiet isolation room, intravenous benzodiazepines (diazepam) and neuromuscular blockers.
5. Active immunization: Tetanus toxoid vaccine given at a different site (clinical disease does not confer immunity).

**Tag:** `Department, Microbiology 2026`

### Q38: Explain the concept of Herd Immunity achieved through the Sabin oral polio vaccine.
**Model Answer:**
Following oral immunization, the live attenuated vaccine virus replicates in the vaccinated child's gastrointestinal tract and is shed in feces into the environment. Fecal-oral spread of the attenuated virus to non-immunized community contacts results in indirect, passive immunization of contacts, establishing widespread herd immunity and interrupting wild poliovirus transmission.

**Tag:** `Department, Microbiology 2026`

### Q39: Why is environmental hygiene and sanitation essential in controlling arboviral diseases?
**Model Answer:**
Because mosquito vectors breed in artificial containers, open water reservoirs, and stagnant water pools near human dwellings. Environmental cleaning, eliminating standing water, and sanitation disrupt mosquito oviposition and larval development, drastically reducing vector population density.

### Q40: Why can a patient bitten by a rabid animal successfully benefit from active post-exposure vaccination?
**Model Answer:**
Rabies is unique because the virus has a relatively long incubation period (typically 1 to 3 months) while replicating slowly in peripheral muscle tissue before gaining access to peripheral nerves and travelling to the CNS. This extended window provides sufficient time for the post-exposure active vaccine series to stimulate robust neutralizing antibodies that clear the virus before it penetrates the central nervous system.

### Q43: What is neonatal tetanus (tetanus neonatorum), what is its cause, and how is it prevented?
**Model Answer:**
Cause: Contamination of the freshly cut umbilical cord stump with Clostridium tetani endospores during unhygienic home delivery or improper traditional dressing.
Prevention: 1. Active immunization of pregnant women with tetanus toxoid (maternal IgG crosses placenta to protect neonate). 2. Hygienic delivery practices ("clean hands, clean surface, clean cord tie and cut"). 3. Application of topical antiseptic (e.g. 7.1% chlorhexidine) to the umbilical cord stump.

**Tag:** `Department, Microbiology 2026`

### Q44: Why is it strictly contraindicated to feed honey or corn syrup to infants under one year of age?
**Model Answer:**
Honey frequently contains Clostridium botulinum endospores. Because an infant's intestinal flora and gastric acidity are underdeveloped, ingested spores can germinate, colonize the lumen of the large intestine, and produce botulinum neurotoxin in vivo, resulting in infant botulism ("floppy baby syndrome"), progressive flaccid paralysis, respiratory failure, and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).

**Tag:** `Department, Microbiology 2026`

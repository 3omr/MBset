# Histology Department Book Questions 2026 (Written)

### Q1: Explain the structure of a chromosome and the significance of the centromere

**Model Answer:**
• Structure: Each chromosome is formed by two sister chromatids joined together at a constriction point called the centromere. The centromere divides each chromatid into two arms: a short arm (p arm) and a long arm (q arm).
• Significance of Centromere: The centromere gives each chromosome its characteristic morphological shape and contains kinetochores where spindle microtubules attach during cell division, ensuring accurate chromosome segregation to daughter cells.

### Q2: Discuss the difference between genotype and phenotype with examples

**Model Answer:**
• Genotype: The specific genetic makeup or nucleotide sequence of an organism inherited from its parents (e.g., homozygous dominant BB, heterozygous Bb, homozygous recessive bb, or 46,XY).
• Phenotype: The observable physical, biochemical, or physiological expression of that genotype as influenced by environmental factors (e.g., brown eyes vs blue eyes, normal vs sickle cell hemoglobin).

---

### Q3: Describe the process of karyotyping and its importance in medical genetics

**Model Answer:**
• Process:
  1. Peripheral blood is collected in an anticoagulant tube (heparin).
  2. Mononuclear lymphocytes are separated and cultured in growth medium with phytohemagglutinin (PHA) to stimulate mitotic division.
  3. After 48-72 hours, colchicine is added to arrest dividing cells in metaphase by disrupting spindle microtubules.
  4. Cells are treated with a hypotonic solution to swell nuclei, fixed, spread on slides, and stained (e.g., Giemsa banding).
  5. Metaphase spreads are photographed and arranged in homologous pairs.
• Importance: Detects numerical chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., Down syndrome, Turner syndrome) and major structural aberrations (translocations, deletions, inversions).

---

### Q4: What are alleles, and how do they contribute to genetic diversity

**Model Answer:**
• Alleles are alternative nucleotide sequence variants of the same gene located at the same locus on homologous chromosomes.
• Contribution to Diversity: Different alleles produce phenotypic variation among individuals. During meiosis, crossing over and independent assortment generate novel combinations of maternal and paternal alleles, providing evolutionary adaptability.

---

### Q5: Explain the classification of chromosomes based on their length and centromere position

**Model Answer:**
Chromosomes are classified into 7 groups (A through G) based on overall size and centromere position:
1. Metacentric: Centromere is positioned centrally, resulting in arms of approximately equal length (p ≈ q).
2. Submetacentric: Centromere is positioned off-center, creating a distinctly shorter p arm and longer q arm.
3. Acrocentric: Centromere is located very close to one end, producing very short p arms often with satellite stalks (e.g., chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, 22).
4. Telocentric: Centromere is located at the very terminus (not found in normal human karyotypes).

---

### Q6: What is the role of cytogenetics in medical genetics

**Model Answer:**
Cytogenetics investigates chromosome structure, number, and behavior in relation to human pathology. It plays an indispensable role in diagnosing congenital malformations, intellectual disabilities, ambiguous genitalia, recurrent miscarriages, and hematologic malignancies (e.g., Philadelphia chromosome in CML).

---

### Q7: Describe the relationship between genes, proteins, and traits

**Model Answer:**
Genes are specific segments of DNA encoding functional polypeptides or RNAs. Through transcription (DNA to mRNA) and translation (mRNA to protein), the genetic code directs the synthesis of structural proteins, enzymes, and signaling molecules. These proteins determine cellular functions and visible physiological traits.

---

### Q8: Discuss the implications of genetic testing in modern medicine

**Model Answer:**
1. Diagnostic testing: Confirms or rules out suspected monogenic disorders in symptomatic patients.
2. Presymptomatic & predictive testing: Identifies predispositions to late-onset conditions (e.g., BRCA1 in hereditary breast cancer).
3. Carrier screening: Assesses recurrence risks for prospective parents.
4. Pharmacogenomics: Tailors medication choices and dosages according to patient genotype to optimize efficacy and avoid toxicity.

---

### Q9: Discuss the different types of genetic diseases and provide examples for each

**Model Answer:**
1. Chromosomal Disorders: Caused by numerical or structural chromosome aberrations (e.g., Down syndrome 47,XX,+21; Turner syndrome 45,X).
2. Monogenic (Mendelian) Disorders: Caused by single gene mutations (e.g., Sickle cell anemia, Cystic fibrosis, Phenylketonuria).
3. Multifactorial (Complex) Disorders: Result from interactions between multiple susceptibility genes and environmental triggers (e.g., Diabetes mellitus, Hypertension, Cleft lip).
4. Mitochondrial Disorders: Caused by mutations in maternally inherited mtDNA (e.g., Leber hereditary optic neuropathy).

---

### Q10: Explain the mechanisms behind chromosomal numerical abnormalities

**Model Answer:**
1. Non-disjunction: Failure of homologous chromosomes to separate during meiosis I or sister chromatids during meiosis II, generating aneuploid gametes (monosomy or trisomy).
2. Anaphase lag: Delay in chromosome movement during anaphase, causing a chromosome to be excluded from the newly formed nuclear envelope.
3. Polyploidy: Fertilization of an ovum by two sperm (dispermy) or failure of meiotic division, resulting in entire extra haploid sets (triploidy 3n = 69).

---

### Q11: What is the role of crossing over during meiosis

**Model Answer:**
Crossing over occurs during pachytene of prophase I between non-sister chromatids of homologous pairs. It facilitates reciprocal exchange of genetic material at chiasmata, producing unique non-parental allele combinations that drive genetic diversity and ensure proper chromosome segregation.

---

### Q12: Analyze the impact of Klinefelter syndrome on male development

**Model Answer:**
Klinefelter syndrome (typically 47,XXY) is characterized by testicular dysgenesis, leading to primary hypogonadism, low testosterone, and elevated gonadotropins (FSH and LH). Clinical manifestations include gynecomastia, eunuchoid tall stature with elongated extremities, sparse body hair, azoospermia/infertility, and potential mild educational difficulties.

---

### Q13: Explain the significance of the haploid number in sexual reproduction

**Model Answer:**
The production of haploid gametes (n = 23) via meiotic reduction division ensures that upon syngamy (fertilization), the diploid species chromosome number (2n = 46) is faithfully restored in the zygote across successive generations, preventing chromosome doubling.

---

### Q14: Describe the consequences of chromosomal structural abnormalities

**Model Answer:**
Structural abnormalities (deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations) disrupt normal gene dosage and architecture. Deletions cause haploinsufficiency and dysmorphic syndromes (e.g., Cri-du-chat 5p-); duplications produce partial trisomies; translocations may generate oncogenic fusion proteins (e.g., BCR-ABL) or increase miscarriage risks in balanced carriers.

---

### Q15: Discuss the importance of genetic counseling for families with a history of genetic disorders

**Model Answer:**
Genetic counseling provides education, risk assessment, and psychosocial support. It calculates recurrence risks, explains inheritance patterns, coordinates carrier/prenatal testing options, and empowers families to make informed reproductive and healthcare decisions.

---

### Q16: Explain the process of aneuploidy and its potential effects on human development

**Model Answer:**
Aneuploidy is the gain or loss of individual chromosomes from the normal diploid set (e.g., 2n+1 or 2n-1). It disrupts normal gene dosage balance, resulting in developmental arrest, spontaneous miscarriage, or severe congenital anomalies and cognitive delays in surviving individuals.

### Q19: Enumerate Stages of the prophase 1 of 1st meiotic division

**Model Answer:**
1. Leptotene (chromatin condensation into thin threads)
2. Zygotene (synapsis of homologous chromosomes forming synaptonemal complexes)
3. Pachytene (crossing over and genetic recombination at chiasmata)
4. Diplotene (chiasmata become visible as homologs begin separating)
5. Diakinesis (terminalization of chiasmata and nuclear membrane breakdown)

### Q20: Enumerate Types of chromosome according to position of centromere

**Model Answer:**
1. Metacentric (centromere at center; p = q)
2. Submetacentric (centromere off-center; p < q)
3. Acrocentric (centromere near end with satellites; p << q)
4. Telocentric (centromere at the terminal end)

### Q21: Define Apoptosis

**Model Answer:**
Programmed, energy-dependent cell death characterized by cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and formation of apoptotic bodies without inducing an inflammatory reaction.

---

### Q22: Define Cell Cycle

**Model Answer:**
The ordered series of events through which a cell grows, replicates its genome, and divides into two genetically identical daughter cells. Comprises Interphase (G1, S, G2) and M phase.

---

### Q23: Define Mitosis

**Model Answer:**
The process of somatic nuclear division in eukaryotic cells that segregates duplicated chromosomes equally into two daughter nuclei, maintaining the diploid chromosome number.

---

### Q24: Define Karyotyping

**Model Answer:**
The laboratory technique of visualizing, ordering, and pairing all metaphase chromosomes of an organism according to size, banding patterns, and centromere location.

---

### Q25: Describe the phases of the cell cycle and their significance

**Model Answer:**
• Interphase:
  - G1 phase: Cellular growth, organelle duplication, and RNA/protein synthesis.
  - S phase: Semi-conservative replication of nuclear DNA and centrosome duplication.
  - G2 phase: Synthesis of mitotic proteins (tubulin), accumulation of ATP, and final preparation for division.
• M phase: Nuclear division (mitosis) and cytoplasmic division (cytokinesis).
• Significance: Preserves genetic stability and coordinates tissue growth, maintenance, and repair.

---

### Q26: Discuss the significance of the G0 phase in the cell cycle

**Model Answer:**
G0 is a quiescent, non-dividing state where cells exit the active cell cycle. Cells in G0 perform specialized metabolic tasks. Some remain permanently in G0 (terminally differentiated neurons, cardiac myocytes), while facultative dividers (hepatocytes) can re-enter G1 upon growth factor stimulation.

---

### Q27: Explain the role of checkpoints in the cell cycle

**Model Answer:**
Checkpoints are surveillance mechanisms that monitor fidelity of critical events:
1. G1/S Checkpoint (Restriction point): Evaluates cell size, nutrient availability, and DNA integrity before committing to replication.
2. G2/M Checkpoint: Assesses complete and undamaged DNA replication prior to mitosis.
3. Spindle Assembly Checkpoint (M Checkpoint): Ensures all kinetochores are properly attached to spindle microtubules before sister chromatid separation at anaphase.

---

### Q28: What are the consequences of mutations in cell cycle regulatory proteins

**Model Answer:**
Mutations in tumor suppressor genes (e.g., TP53, RB1) or gain-of-function oncogenic mutations in cyclins/CDKs abolish checkpoint surveillance. Cells divide unchecked despite harboring severe DNA lesions, driving genomic instability, clonal expansion, and malignant neoplasia.

---

### Q29: Describe the process of DNA replication during the S phase

**Model Answer:**
Replication begins at multiple replication origins where helicase unwinds DNA. Primase synthesizes RNA primers, and DNA polymerases synthesize leading (continuous) and lagging (Okazaki fragments) strands in the 5' to 3' direction. RNA primers are removed, gaps filled, and nicks sealed by DNA ligase, doubling DNA content from 2n to 4n.

---

### Q30: How do external factors influence the cell cycle

**Model Answer:**
Extracellular mitogens, growth factors (e.g., PDGF, EGF), and hormones bind to cell surface tyrosine kinase receptors, triggering intracellular MAPK signaling cascades that induce cyclin D synthesis and overcome the G1 restriction point. Conversely, contact inhibition and inhibitory cytokines (TGF-beta) halt progression.

---

### Q31: Explain the role of cyclins in the regulation of the cell cycle

**Model Answer:**
Cyclins are regulatory proteins whose intracellular concentrations oscillate periodically throughout the cycle. They bind and activate Cyclin-Dependent Kinases (CDKs). The active cyclin-CDK complexes phosphorylate key target substrates (such as Rb), driving transitions between successive cycle phases.

---

### Q32: What is the relationship between the cell cycle and cancer

**Model Answer:**
Cancer is fundamentally a disease of dysregulated cell cycle control. Loss of negative regulators (CDK inhibitors like p16, p21, p27, and p53) or constitutive activation of proto-oncogenes permits bypass of normal arrest checkpoints, resulting in limitless replicative potential and tumor progression.

---

### Q33: Discuss the importance of apoptosis in the context of the cell cycle

**Model Answer:**
Apoptosis acts as an indispensable cellular quality control mechanism. When irreparable DNA damage or spindle defects are detected at cell cycle checkpoints, pro-apoptotic pathways (Bax/Bak, caspase cascades) are triggered, eliminating dangerously mutated cells and preventing oncogenesis.

---

### Q34: What are the implications of reduced p27 levels in cancer prognosis

**Model Answer:**
p27 is an essential CDK inhibitor that arrests cells in G1. Loss or downregulation of p27 removes cell cycle brakes, accelerating proliferation. In oncology, low p27 expression correlates strongly with high histological tumor grade, lymph node metastasis, aggressive tumor behavior, and poor clinical prognosis.

---

### Q35: How does the cell cycle differ in prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

**Model Answer:**
• Prokaryotes: Lack a membrane-bound nucleus and undergo rapid, continuous binary fission without distinct G1, S, G2 phases; DNA replication and cell division occur simultaneously.
• Eukaryotes: Feature compartmentalized genomes and complex regulation through sequential G1, S, G2, and M phases, regulated by cyclins, CDKs, and spindle apparatus.

---

### Q36: Describe the stages of mitosis and their significance

**Model Answer:**
1. Prophase: Chromatin condenses into distinct chromosomes; centrosomes migrate to opposite poles; mitotic spindle begins assembly.
2. Metaphase: Nuclear envelope dissolves; chromosomes align symmetrically along the equatorial metaphase plate.
3. Anaphase: Centromeres cleave; sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite spindle poles by shortening kinetochore microtubules.
4. Telophase: Nuclear envelopes reassemble around separated chromatid sets; chromosomes de-condense; followed by cytokinesis.
• Significance: Generates two genetically identical diploid daughter cells, ensuring tissue growth, development, and genomic continuity.

---

### Q37: Define and differentiate between pluripotent and multipotent stem cells, providing an example for each

**Model Answer:**
• Pluripotent Stem Cells: Primitive stem cells capable of differentiating into cells derived from all three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm), but cannot form extra-embryonic tissues (placenta). Example: Embryonic stem cells (inner cell mass of blastocyst).
• Multipotent Stem Cells: Lineage-restricted stem cells capable of giving rise only to multiple cell types within a specific tissue or family. Example: Hematopoietic stem cells (giving rise to all blood cell lineages).

---

### Q38: Describe the morphological changes a cell undergoes during apoptosis

**Model Answer:**
1. Cell shrinkage and loss of cell-cell contacts.
2. Chromatin condensation (pyknosis) and marginalization beneath the nuclear envelope.
3. Internucleosomal DNA fragmentation into 180-200 bp oligomers (karyorrhexis).
4. Plasma membrane blebbing.
5. Budding into membrane-enclosed apoptotic bodies containing preserved organelles.
6. Rapid phagocytosis by macrophages without release of intracellular contents or inflammatory reaction.

---

### Q39: Discuss two medical applications of stem cells in regenerative medicine

**Model Answer:**
1. Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation: Bone marrow or cord blood stem cell transplants used to reconstitute healthy hematopoietic and immune systems in patients with leukemia, lymphoma, or aplastic anemia.
2. Tissue Regeneration and Cellular Therapy: Research and clinical trials utilizing mesenchymal or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to generate dopamine-producing neurons for Parkinson's disease, cardiomyocyte patches for ischemic heart failure, and insulin-producing beta-islet cells for type 1 diabetes.

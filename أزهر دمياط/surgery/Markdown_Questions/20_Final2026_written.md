### Q1: A 48-year-old female presents with a breast mass in the right outer quadrant associated with axillary swelling. Her mother died of breast cancer. a) What are the risk factors for breast cancer? b) Mention signs of breast malignancy. c) Differentiate between benign and malignant breast lumps.

**Model Answer:** a) Risk factors: Positive family history in 1st degree relatives (BRCA mutations), age >40y, female gender, early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, obesity, hormone replacement therapy. b) Signs of malignancy: Hard irregular lump, tethering/fixation to skin or pectoral fascia, skin dimpling, nipple retraction, peau d'orange, bloody nipple discharge, hard non-tender fixed axillary lymphadenopathy. c) Differentiation: Benign: smooth, well-defined, soft/elastic or firm, freely mobile, non-tender or tender with menses, no skin changes or nipple retraction, normal axillary nodes. Malignant: hard/stony, poorly defined irregular borders, fixed to surrounding tissues, skin tethering/peau d'orange, unilateral bloody discharge, matted fixed axillary nodes.

### Q2: A 45-year-old male presents to the emergency room with severe abdominal pain following a road traffic accident 4 days ago. BP 90/62 mmHg, HR 130 bpm, RR 25/min, Temp 38. 5 C, Hb 7 g/dL, WBC 18, 000/uL. CT scan reveals moderate intra-peritoneal collection and grade II liver laceration. a) Explain what types of shock occurred in this patient. b) Differentiate between SIRS, Sepsis, and Septic Shock. c) Mention the management for this patient.

**Model Answer:** a) Types of shock: 1) Hemorrhagic / Hypovolemic shock (due to ongoing/initial bleeding from liver laceration, Hb 7 g/dL, tachycardia, hypotension), 2) Septic / Distributive shock (due to infected intraperitoneal collection after 4 days delay, fever 38. 5 C, WBC 18, 000). b) Differentiation: SIRS: 2 or more of: Temp >38 or <36 C, HR >90, RR >20, WBC >12, 000 or <4, 000. Sepsis: SIRS criteria + documented or suspected infection source. Septic Shock: Sepsis + persistent hypotension (MAP <65 mmHg) requiring vasopressors despite adequate fluid resuscitation, and serum lactate >2 mmol/L. c) Management: ABCs, supplemental high-flow oxygen, 2 large bore IV lines, aggressive fluid resuscitation with warmed crystalloids and blood products (PRBCs, FFP, platelets), broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, immediate abdominal exploration / laparotomy or angioembolization and percutaneous drainage of collection.

### Q3: A 60-year-old male presents with an irreducible inguinal swelling associated with repeated vomiting, absolute constipation, and past history of right inguinal herniorrhaphy. On examination there is a red, tender, and hot swelling in the right inguinal region. BP 110/60 mmHg, HR 100 bpm, RR 20/min, Temp 38 C. a) What is the most probable diagnosis? b) What are the risk factors for wound/hernia recurrence and failure? c) Mention management for this patient. d) Mention differential diagnosis of scrotal swellings.

**Model Answer:** a) Diagnosis: Recurrent strangulated right inguinal hernia with intestinal obstruction. b) Risk factors for recurrence/wound failure: Patient factors: increased intra-abdominal pressure (chronic cough, BPH, constipation), smoking, obesity, malnutrition, uncontrolled diabetes, steroid therapy. Surgical factors: wound infection, inadequate hernia repair / missed sac, excessive tension on repair, inadequate mesh overlap. c) Management: Emergency resuscitation: NPO, NG tube decompression, IV fluids, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, analgesia, emergency groin exploration with laparotomy if gangrenous bowel: open sac, assess bowel viability, resect gangrenous bowel and perform end-to-end anastomosis, primary tissue repair without prosthetic mesh. d) DD of scrotal swellings: Inguinoscrotal hernia, hydrocele, spermatocele, varicocele, testicular torsion, epididymo-orchitis, hematocele, testicular tumor.

### Q4: A 15-year-old male presents with a lateral neck swelling of 4 weeks duration associated with intermittent fever, weight loss, and splenomegaly, not responding to antibiotic treatment. a) What is the most probable diagnosis? b) Describe boundaries of neck triangles. c) Enumerate investigations needed to reach diagnosis. d) Enumerate differential diagnosis of this swelling.

**Model Answer:** a) Diagnosis: Lymphoma (Hodgkin or Non-Hodgkin lymphoma) or Tuberculous lymphadenitis. b) Neck Triangles: Anterior triangle: bounded by anterior border of sternocleidomastoid (posteriorly), midline of neck (anteriorly), inferior border of mandible (superiorly). Posterior triangle: bounded by posterior border of sternocleidomastoid (anteriorly), anterior border of trapezius (posteriorly), middle third of clavicle (inferiorly). c) Investigations: CBC with differential and ESR, excisional lymph node biopsy (gold standard for histopathology and immunohistochemistry, identifying Reed-Sternberg cells), contrast CT neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis for staging, bone marrow biopsy, tuberculin skin test / IGRA. d) Differential diagnosis: Reactive bacterial lymphadenitis, infectious mononucleosis, tuberculosis (scrofula), metastatic carcinoma, branchial cleft cyst, carotid body tumor.

### Q5: A 56-year-old male presents complaining of progressive pain in his left foot for 3 months. Pain initially occurred only on walking and was relieved by rest, but now occurs after 50 meters and also in toes at night. Heavy smoker for 15 years. Toes pale with small painful ulcer on tip of big toe. Left pedal pulses weak/absent. a) What is the most likely diagnosis? b) Mention three factors in this case that support your diagnosis. c) What investigations will confirm diagnosis? d) Outline the principles of management.

**Model Answer:** a) Diagnosis: Severe Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD) / Critical Limb Ischemia (CLI) secondary to Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans) or Atherosclerosis. b) Supporting factors: 1) Heavy smoking history, 2) Progression from intermittent claudication to ischemic rest pain (worse at night, relieved by dependency), 3) Ischemic signs: pale foot, painful tip ulcer, weak/absent pedal pulses. c) Investigations: Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI <0. 5), Arterial Duplex ultrasound of lower extremities, CT Angiography (CTA) or Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA), Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA) showing segmental occlusions ('corkscrew collaterals' in Buerger's). d) Principles of management: 1) Mandatory complete smoking cessation, 2) Pain management, 3) Wound care of ischemic ulcer, 4) Pharmacotherapy: antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), statin, cilostazol, 5) Revascularization: endovascular balloon angioplasty/stenting or surgical arterial bypass grafting (femoro-distal), 6) Amputation of gangrenous toe if irreversible.

### Q6: Regarding circumferential burns: a) Definition? b) Complications? c) Treatment?

**Model Answer:** a) Definition: A full-thickness or deep partial-thickness burn that completely encircles an anatomical region (limb, chest, or neck). b) Complications: In limbs: Tourniquet effect due to rigid unyielding eschar + tissue edema resulting in vascular compromise, acute compartment syndrome, nerve compression, tissue necrosis, and loss of extremity. In chest: Restriction of chest wall excursion leading to severe respiratory distress, hypoventilation, and asphyxia. In neck: Tracheal compression and acute airway compromise. c) Treatment: Immediate emergency Escharotomy: full-thickness longitudinal surgical incisions through the non-elastic eschar into the subcutaneous fat to release constriction and restore tissue perfusion or chest excursion, along mid-lateral/medial axes avoiding major superficial nerves and vessels. Performed at bedside without anesthesia (full-thickness burn is insensitive). If pressure remains elevated, fasciotomy is required.

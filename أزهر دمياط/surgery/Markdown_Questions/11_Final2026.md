### Q1: 45 years old female presented with breast mass at the right outer quadrant associated with axillary swelling, her mother died with breast cancer. (9Marks) A. What are risk factors for breast cancer? B. Mention signs of breast cancer? C. Differentiate between benign and malignant neoplasms in general?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** A. Risk factors for breast cancer: female sex and increasing age; family history in first-degree relatives (as her mother) and BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations; previous breast cancer or atypical hyperplasia (ductal/lobular) and LCIS; prolonged estrogen exposure: early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, first full-term pregnancy after 30 years, no breast feeding, hormone replacement therapy and oral contraceptives; obesity after menopause, alcohol intake, high-fat diet; previous chest wall irradiation; high socioeconomic class and Western lifestyle.
B. Signs of breast cancer: hard, irregular, painless lump, commonly in the upper outer quadrant, with ill-defined edge; fixation to skin (skin dimpling, peau d'orange, ulceration, satellite nodules) or to deep structures (pectoral fascia, chest wall); nipple retraction or deviation, bloody nipple discharge, eczematous nipple changes (Paget's disease); axillary lymph nodes that are hard, discrete, later matted or fixed, and supraclavicular nodes; arm lymphedema; signs of distant metastases (bone pain and pathological fractures, hepatomegaly and jaundice, pleural effusion, neurological deficits); cachexia.
C. Benign versus malignant neoplasms: benign tumors grow slowly, are well differentiated, encapsulated and well defined, grow by expansion, do not invade or metastasize, rarely recur after excision and have little systemic effect (except pressure or hormone secretion); malignant tumors grow rapidly, range from well to poorly differentiated (anaplasia, pleomorphism, hyperchromatism, abnormal mitoses), are non-encapsulated with ill-defined edges, infiltrate and destroy surrounding tissues, spread by lymphatics, blood and implantation (metastasis), commonly recur after excision, and cause systemic effects (cachexia, anemia, paraneoplastic syndromes) and may be fatal.

### Q2: Male patient 35y old presented to emergency room with acute abdominal pain With history of road traffic accident 4 days ago, blood pressure: 90/60 mm.hg., pulse: 130 b/min, respiratory rate: 25 c/min, temperature: 38.5C, hemoglobin: 7 g/l, white blood cell count: 18000, computed tomography (CT) scan revealed moderate amount of intra peritoneal collection and grade II liver laceration. (9Marks) A. Explain what are types of shock was happened for this patient? B. Differentiate between systemic inflammatory response, sepsis, and septic shock? C. Mention management for this patient?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** A. Types of shock: initially hypovolemic (hemorrhagic) shock from blood loss due to the grade II liver laceration with intraperitoneal collection (hypotension 90/60, tachycardia 130, tachypnea, hemoglobin 7 g/l); now, 4 days later, with fever 38.5C and leukocytosis 18000, septic shock is superimposed (infected intraperitoneal collection / peritonitis), so it is a mixed hypovolemic and septic shock.
B. SIRS: systemic inflammatory response to any insult (infective or not) with two or more of: temperature above 38C or below 36C, heart rate above 90/min, respiratory rate above 20/min or PaCO2 below 32 mmHg, WBC above 12000 or below 4000/mm3 or more than 10% immature bands. Sepsis: SIRS with a documented or suspected source of infection (now defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, SOFA increase of 2 or more). Septic shock: sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to keep mean arterial pressure at 65 mmHg or more and serum lactate above 2 mmol/l despite adequate fluid resuscitation.
C. Management: ABC resuscitation (ATLS), oxygen, two large-bore IV cannulae, blood sampling for grouping and cross-matching, CBC, coagulation, renal and liver functions, lactate and blood cultures; fluid resuscitation with crystalloids and blood transfusion to correct hemoglobin; urinary catheter and CVP monitoring of output; broad-spectrum IV antibiotics started early; drainage of the infected intraperitoneal collection (CT/US-guided percutaneous drainage or laparotomy/laparoscopy with peritoneal lavage), and control of liver bleeding (conservative management for stable grade II injury, angioembolization, or operative hemostasis if unstable); vasopressors (noradrenaline) if hypotension persists after fluids; ICU care, correction of acidosis and electrolytes, nutritional support, DVT and stress ulcer prophylaxis.

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** A. Most probable diagnosis: strangulated (obstructed) recurrent right inguinal hernia (irreducible, with intestinal obstruction features: vomiting and absolute constipation; red, tender, hot swelling with fever and tachycardia).
B. Risk factors for wound failure (and hernia recurrence): patient factors: old age, obesity, malnutrition and hypoproteinemia, anemia, diabetes mellitus, smoking, jaundice, uremia, malignancy, steroid or cytotoxic therapy, chronic cough, constipation or urinary straining raising intra-abdominal pressure; local and technical factors: wound infection, hematoma or seroma, poor blood supply, tension on the repair, improper suture material or technique, poor tissue quality, and early strenuous activity.

Management: urgent admission, nil by mouth, nasogastric tube suction, IV fluids and correction of electrolytes, IV antibiotics, analgesia, urinary catheter; no attempt at forcible reduction (taxis); emergency surgical exploration: open the sac and inspect the contents; if bowel is viable, reduce it; if gangrenous, resect with anastomosis; ligate the sac and repair the defect (tissue repair such as herniorrhaphy in a contaminated field, or mesh hernioplasty when the field is clean); postoperative care and treatment of predisposing factors.
D. Differential diagnosis of scrotal swellings: inguinoscrotal hernia; hydrocele (vaginal, congenital, infantile, encysted hydrocele of the cord); hematocele; varicocele; epididymal cyst and spermatocele; acute epididymo-orchitis and chronic (tuberculous) epididymitis; torsion of the testis; testicular tumor; gumma of the testis; scrotal skin lesions (sebaceous cysts, carcinoma of the scrotum, Fournier's gangrene); lipoma of the cord.

### Q4: Male patient 15years old presented with lateral neck swelling 4 weeks duration associated with intermittent fever, weight loss, splenomegaly and not responding to antibiotic treatment. (9 Marks) A. What is the most probable diagnosis? B. Disciple boundaries of neck triangles? C. Enumerate the investigations needed to reach diagnosis? D. Enumerate differential diagnosis of this swelling?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** A. Most probable diagnosis: lymphoma, most likely Hodgkin's lymphoma (young patient, lateral neck lymphadenopathy with B symptoms - fever, weight loss - and splenomegaly, not responding to antibiotics).
B. Boundaries of neck triangles: anterior triangle: anteriorly the midline of the neck, posteriorly the anterior border of sternomastoid, superiorly (base) the lower border of the mandible; it is subdivided into submental, submandibular (digastric), carotid and muscular triangles. Posterior triangle: anteriorly the posterior border of sternomastoid, posteriorly the anterior border of trapezius, inferiorly (base) the middle third of the clavicle, apex at the meeting of sternomastoid and trapezius at the superior nuchal line; roof is the investing layer of deep cervical fascia, floor is the prevertebral fascia over the muscles (splenius capitis, levator scapulae, scalenes); the inferior belly of omohyoid divides it into occipital and supraclavicular (subclavian) triangles.
C. Investigations: CBC with differential, ESR, LDH, liver and renal functions; neck ultrasound; excisional lymph node biopsy for histopathology and immunohistochemistry (Reed-Sternberg cells, CD15/CD30) - FNAC is not sufficient; tuberculin test and chest X-ray to exclude tuberculosis; staging by CT of neck, chest, abdomen and pelvis or PET-CT; bone marrow biopsy when indicated; abdominal ultrasound for splenomegaly.
D. Differential diagnosis: lymph node causes: tuberculous lymphadenitis, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, infectious mononucleosis and other viral infections, toxoplasmosis, leukemia, sarcoidosis, secondary deposits, HIV; non-nodal lateral neck swellings: branchial cyst, cystic hygroma, carotid body tumor, neurofibroma, lipoma, sternomastoid tumor, cold abscess.

### Q5: 34-year-old male presents to the outpatient clinic complaining of progressive pain in his right foot for the last 3 months. The pain initially occurred only on walking for long distances but now occurs after walking about 50 meters and is relieved by rest. For the last 2 weeks, he has also noticed pain in his toes even at rest, especially at night. He is a heavy smoker (20 cigarettes/day for 15 years). He has no history of diabetes mellitus or hypertension. On examination: The right foot pale with small painful ulcer on the tip of the big toe. Dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses on the right side are weak and difficult to palpate. Sensation is intact and motor power is normal. (9 Marks) A. What is the most likely diagnosis? B. Which factors in this case support your diagnosis? (Mention three)? C. What investigations will help to confirm the diagnosis? D. Outline the principles of management in this patient?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** A. Most likely diagnosis: chronic lower limb ischemia (critical limb ischemia) of the right lower limb due to Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans), presenting with intermittent claudication progressing to rest pain and ischemic ulceration.
B. Supporting factors: young male (34 years); heavy smoker (20 cigarettes/day for 15 years); no diabetes or hypertension (atherosclerosis less likely); distal (infrapopliteal) disease with weak dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses; progression from claudication (claudication distance 50 meters) to rest pain at night and a painful ulcer on the tip of the big toe with a pale foot.
C. Investigations: ankle brachial pressure index; arterial duplex ultrasound; CT angiography or conventional angiography (segmental occlusion of small and medium distal arteries with corkscrew collaterals and normal proximal arteries); investigations to exclude other causes: blood sugar, lipid profile, autoimmune and coagulation screens, echocardiography to exclude embolic source.
D. Principles of management: complete and permanent cessation of smoking (the most important measure); foot care, treatment of the ulcer and infection, analgesia; supervised exercise; drugs: antiplatelets, vasodilators such as prostacyclin analogues (iloprost), cilostazol; lumbar sympathectomy for rest pain and superficial ulcers; revascularization (bypass) rarely possible because of distal disease; conservative amputation of the gangrenous toe when needed, and major amputation for extensive gangrene.

### Q6: Regarding Circumferential burn mention the followings. (11 Marks) A. Definition? B. Complications? C. Treatment?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** A. Definition: a full-thickness (deep) burn involving the whole circumference of a limb, the chest, the neck or a digit, producing a rigid inelastic eschar that acts as a tourniquet as edema accumulates under it.
B. Complications: limbs - progressive venous then arterial obstruction with compartment syndrome, distal ischemia, nerve and muscle necrosis, gangrene and loss of the limb, rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury; chest - restriction of chest wall movement with hypoventilation, respiratory failure and hypoxia; neck - compression of the airway and venous return; later - infection and sepsis, and circumferential contracture with deformity and hypertrophic scarring.
C. Treatment: general burn resuscitation (ABC, fluid resuscitation by Parkland formula, analgesia, tetanus prophylaxis, monitoring of urine output); elevation of the limb and monitoring of distal circulation (pulses, capillary refill, Doppler, compartment pressure) and ventilation; urgent escharotomy - longitudinal incisions through the full thickness of the eschar along the midlateral and midmedial lines of the limb (and digits), or along the anterior axillary lines with a transverse subcostal incision for the chest, until the tissues bulge and circulation or ventilation improves; fasciotomy if compartment syndrome persists; followed by excision of dead tissue and skin grafting, physiotherapy and splinting to prevent contractures.

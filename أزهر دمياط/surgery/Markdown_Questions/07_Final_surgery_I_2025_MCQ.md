### Q33: Define shock and describe the resuscitation and management of hypovolemic shock?

**Model Answer:** Definition: A state of acute, systemic tissue hypoperfusion resulting in inadequate cellular oxygen delivery and energy failure. Management: 1) Secure Airway and High-flow Oxygen (ABCs). 2) Immediate control of external bleeding with direct pressure. 3) Establish two large-bore peripheral IV lines (16G or 14G). 4) Fluid resuscitation with warmed balanced crystalloids (Ringer's lactate or normal saline) 1-2 liters. 5) Early administration of blood products (packed RBCs, FFP, platelets in 1: 1: 1 ratio) for hemorrhagic shock class III/IV. 6) Monitor response: urine output (>0. 5 mL/kg/h), heart rate, blood pressure, mental status, and serial lactate/base deficit. 7) Definitive surgical or angiographic hemostasis.

### Q38: What is the differential diagnosis of acute painful scrotal swelling? Mention the clinical picture, investigation, and treatment of one of them.

**Model Answer:** Differential diagnoses: 1) Torsion of the spermatic cord (testicular torsion), 2) Acute epididymo-orchitis, 3) Torsion of testicular appendage (hydatid of Morgagni), 4) Traumatic hematocele / testicular rupture, 5) Strangulated inguinoscrotal hernia, 6) Henoch-Schonlein purpura / acute idiopathic scrotal edema. Testicular Torsion: Clinical picture: Sudden severe unilateral scrotal pain, swelling, nausea/vomiting, elevated high-riding horizontal testis, absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn sign (pain not relieved by elevation). Investigation: Color Doppler ultrasound of the scrotum shows absent/diminished testicular blood flow (urgent; must not delay surgery). Treatment: Immediate surgical exploration via scrotal incision within 6 hours: detorsion, evaluation of viability with warm packs; if viable, bilateral orchiopexy (fixing both testes to prevent contralateral torsion); if non-viable, orchiectomy and contralateral orchiopexy.

### Q39: A 22-year-old female accidentally discovered a painless solitary lump in the upper lateral quadrant of her left breast. History is irrelevant. On examination, it was firm, smooth, and freely mobile ('breast mouse'). How to investigate, differential diagnosis, and management based on the most probable diagnosis?

**Model Answer:** Most probable diagnosis: Fibroadenoma. Investigations (Triple Assessment): 1) Clinical breast examination, 2) Breast ultrasound (demonstrates well-circumscribed, oval, hypoechoic solid mass wider than tall with smooth margins), 3) Core needle biopsy or FNAC confirming benign fibroepithelial lesion. Differential diagnosis: 1) Breast cyst, 2) Phyllodes tumor (benign/malignant), 3) Fibrocystic change / localized fibroadenosis, 4) Early invasive breast carcinoma. Management: Conservative observation with periodic follow-up ultrasound if small (<2-3 cm) and confirmed benign. Surgical excision (lumpectomy / enucleation via circumareolar or inframammary incision) if mass is >3 cm (giant fibroadenoma), rapid growth, causing pain or cosmetic deformity, patient anxiety, or suspicion of phyllodes tumor.

### Q40: A 50-year-old male presents with intermittent inguinoscrotal swelling for a long duration. In the previous two days, it became permanent and associated with vomiting. In the last eight hours, it became painful. What is the most probable diagnosis, other differential diagnoses, expected complications, and resuscitation/treatment?

**Model Answer:** Most probable diagnosis: Strangulated indirect inguinal hernia. Differential diagnoses: 1) Obstructed non-strangulated inguinal hernia, 2) Torsion of the testis / spermatic cord, 3) Acute epididymo-orchitis, 4) Tense hydrocele of the cord, 5) Strangulated femoral hernia. Expected complications: Intestinal gangrene, bowel perforation, acute peritonitis, septic shock, multiple organ failure, and death. Resuscitation & Treatment: Immediate emergency surgical management: 1) Resuscitation: NPO, nasogastric tube decompression, IV crystalloid fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (covering Gram-negatives and anaerobes), analgesia, Foley catheter. 2) Emergency herniotomy/herniorrhaphy: Inguinal incision, open hernial sac with care, inspect fluid, secure and assess contents (bowel loop viability: color, peristalsis, mesenteric pulsation). 3) If viable: reduce into abdomen and repair hernia. 4) If non-viable/gangrenous: bowel resection and end-to-end anastomosis, then tissue repair (avoid prosthetic mesh in strangulated contaminated field).

### Q2: Summarize the treatment of hypovolemic shock.

**Model Answer:** 1) Airway and Breathing: High-flow oxygen, ensure clear airway. 2) Immediate control of bleeding with direct pressure or tourniquet. 3) Circulation: Insert two large-bore IV cannulae (14-16G). 4) Rapid fluid resuscitation: Infusion of 1-2 L of warm balanced crystalloids (Ringer's lactate). 5) Early blood transfusion (packed RBCs, FFP, platelets 1: 1: 1) in class III/IV hemorrhage. 6) Monitor vital signs and urine output (>0. 5 mL/kg/h via Foley catheter). 7) Urgent definitive surgical or radiological hemostasis.

### Q3: Enumerate the general and local complications of burns.

**Model Answer:** General complications: Hypovolemic/burn shock, acute renal failure (acute tubular necrosis due to hypoperfusion and hemoglobinuria), inhalation injury and ARDS, burn wound sepsis and septic shock, Curling's stress ulcer, paralytic ileus, hypermetabolic state and weight loss. Local complications: Burn wound infection, conversion of partial to full thickness burn, compartment syndrome from circumferential eschar, hypertrophic scars, keloids, joint contractures, Marjolin's ulcer (squamous cell carcinoma in long-standing burn scar).

### Q5: Compare between a subcutaneous lipoma and a sebaceous cyst (epidermoid cyst).

**Model Answer:** Lipoma: Originates from subcutaneous adipocytes; soft, lobulated swelling with a characteristic 'slippery edge'; overlying skin is completely normal with no punctum and moves freely over the lump; transillumination is usually negative; treated by simple surgical enucleation. Sebaceous Cyst: Arises from hair follicle / epidermoid retention; firm, rounded, smooth, spherical cystic swelling with no slippery edge; attached to the overlying skin at the 'punctum' (central black dot of obstructed orifice); prone to recurrent infection and abscess formation; treated by complete surgical excision including the entire cyst wall capsule and ellipse of skin containing the punctum.

### Q6: Compare between acute lactational mastitis and mastitis carcinomatosa (inflammatory breast carcinoma).

**Model Answer:** Acute Lactational Mastitis: Occurs in young lactating females (especially primiparous during first 6 weeks); acute onset of severe localized throbbing pain, fever, chills, and tender warm fluctuant area with milk engorgement; responsive to antibiotics, milk expression, and drainage if abscess forms. Mastitis Carcinomatosa (Inflammatory Carcinoma): Occurs in older or non-lactating females; rapidly progressing diffuse erythema, warmth, brawny induration involving >one-third of the breast with prominent 'peau d'orange'; no true fever; palpable underlying mass or diffuse breast enlargement; axillary nodes are typically involved; highly aggressive malignancy requiring core needle biopsy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, modified radical mastectomy, and radiotherapy.

### Q10: Describe the clinical picture of deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

**Model Answer:** Symptoms: Unilateral leg pain, tightness, and swelling, aggravated by dependency and walking. Signs: Unilateral pitting edema of calf and ankle, warmth and erythema of the skin, calf tenderness on deep palpation, prominent dilated superficial collateral veins, positive Homans' sign (calf pain on passive dorsiflexion of the ankle, non-specific), positive Neuhof's sign (pain on anteroposterior calf compression), and low-grade fever. In severe cases: Phlegmasia alba dolens (pallor/milk leg) or Phlegmasia cerulea dolens (cyanosis, massive edema, arterial ischemia).

### Q13: Enumerate the contents of the femoral sheath.

**Model Answer:** The femoral sheath is divided into three compartments by fibrous septa: 1) Lateral compartment: Contains the femoral artery and femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve. 2) Intermediate compartment: Contains the femoral vein. 3) Medial compartment (Femoral canal): Contains loose fatty areolar tissue, efferent lymphatics from the deep inguinal nodes, and the lymph node of Cloquet (Rosenmuller). (Note: The femoral nerve lies outside and lateral to the femoral sheath).

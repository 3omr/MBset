### Q1: A 25-year-old male patient presents to the emergency room with a kitchen knife stab in the right calf muscle, with approximately 2000 ml blood loss. a) Enumerate precautions of blood transfusion. b) What types of shock can be expected for this patient? Mention management of them. c) What are the manifestations of tetanus infection if it occurs in this patient?

**Model Answer:** a) Precautions of blood transfusion: 1) Accurate cross-matching and blood group verification (ABO, Rh) at bedside with patient identity check. 2) Inspection of blood bag for hemolysis, expiration date, clots, or abnormal color. 3) Transfusion through a filtered blood administration set. 4) Pre-warming of blood if massive transfusion to prevent hypothermia. 5) Close monitoring of vital signs before, 15 minutes after starting, and periodically. 6) Avoid mixing medications or hypotonic fluids with blood. b) Types of shock & management: 1) Hemorrhagic / Hypovolemic shock (primary): Airway/Breathing/Circulation (ABCs), immediate wound compression/tourniquet, 2 large-bore IV lines, rapid infusion of warmed crystalloids and early uncrossmatched O-neg or type-specific blood (packed RBCs, FFP, platelets 1: 1: 1), urgent surgical exploration for vascular repair. 2) Neurogenic shock: Pain relief, immobilization. 3) Septic shock (delayed if untreated). c) Manifestations of tetanus: Trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus (facial spasm), dysphagia, neck rigidity, opisthotonos (arching of the back), painful reflex spasms triggered by light/noise, and autonomic instability (tachycardia, labile BP, diaphoresis).

### Q2: As regard cancer: a) Definition. b) Etiology. c) Comparison between benign and malignant tumors. d) Diagnostic modalities.

**Model Answer:** a) Definition: An abnormal, uncontrolled mass of tissue growth that exceeds and is uncoordinated with that of normal tissues, persisting after cessation of the initiating stimuli, with capacity to invade and metastasize. b) Etiology: Genetic (oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes like TP53, BRCA), Chemical carcinogens (tobacco, aromatic amines), Physical (radiation, UV light), Biological/Microbial (HPV, EBV, Hepatitis B/C, H. pylori), Hormonal (prolonged estrogen exposure), and Immunological deficiencies. c) Comparison: Benign: Slow growing, encapsulated, well-circumscribed, non-invasive, resembles tissue of origin (well-differentiated), normal mitoses, no metastasis. Malignant: Rapid growing, non-capsulated, infiltrative/invasive locally, pleomorphic/hyperchromatic cells with abnormal mitoses, capable of distant lymph/hematogenous metastasis. d) Diagnosis: 1) Clinical history and physical examination. 2) Imaging (Ultrasound, Mammography, CT, MRI, PET-CT). 3) Endoscopy. 4) Tissue biopsy (FNAC, Core needle biopsy, excisional biopsy - gold standard for histopathology and immunohistochemistry). 5) Serum tumor markers.

### Q3: A 50-year-old male, heavy smoker, presents with a painless cervical lymph node swelling. How can you approach the diagnosis?

**Model Answer:** 1) History: Duration, rate of growth, smoking and alcohol history, systemic B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), primary head and neck symptoms (hoarseness, dysphagia, oral ulcers, sore throat, otalgia, hemoptysis). 2) Physical Examination: Detailed examination of the swelling (size, site, consistency, fixation), comprehensive head & neck ENT exam (oral cavity, tongue, pharynx, larynx, nasopharynx), thyroid examination, other nodal basins, and systemic exam. 3) Diagnostic Workup: Complete blood count (CBC) and ESR, Chest X-ray, Ultrasound of the neck, CT or MRI of head and neck, Panendoscopy (nasopharyngoscopy, laryngoscopy, esophagoscopy), Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) of the node (avoid open incisional biopsy initially to prevent tumor seeding), Core needle biopsy or formal excisional biopsy if FNAC is inconclusive.

### Q4: An obese, diabetic 50-year-old female patient with a past history of right modified radical mastectomy presents with right upper-limb swelling (lymphedema). Outline the management.

**Model Answer:** 1) Preventive / Skin care: Meticulous limb hygiene, avoid skin punctures, injections, blood pressure cuffs on affected arm, prompt treatment of minor cuts and infections with antibiotics to prevent lymphangitis/erysipelas. 2) Complex Decongestive Therapy (CDT - gold standard): Manual lymphatic drainage (MLD) by a trained therapist, multi-layer compression bandaging, daily daytime gradient compression garments (sleeves), and remedial exercises with compression. 3) Intermittent Pneumatic Compression (IPC): Sequential pneumatic pump devices. 4) Weight reduction and strict glycemic control. 5) Pharmacotherapy: Antibiotic prophylaxis (e. g. penicillin) if recurrent cellulitis episodes. 6) Surgical options (for refractory cases): Lymphovenous anastomosis (LVA), vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT), or liposuction / reductive surgery.

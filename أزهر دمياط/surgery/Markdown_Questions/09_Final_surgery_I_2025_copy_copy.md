### Q1: What are the Causes and main clinical symptoms of chronic limb ischemia?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** Chronic limb ischemia is caused mainly by atherosclerotic occlusive disease; less common causes include thromboangiitis obliterans, embolic disease, vasculitis, and aneurysmal disease. Risk factors include smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and older age. Symptoms progress from intermittent claudication to rest pain, often worse at night and relieved by dependency, then ischemic ulceration or gangrene. Examination may show cool pale skin, delayed capillary refill, reduced or absent pulses, dependent rubor, shiny skin, hair loss, and tissue loss.

### Q3: Define shock and describe management of hypovolemic chock?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** Shock is acute circulatory failure causing inadequate tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, with cellular dysfunction and potential organ failure. Hypovolemic shock is managed by immediate assessment and resuscitation: secure the airway and give oxygen, control external or internal bleeding, obtain two large-bore IV lines, send blood for crossmatch and investigations, and rapidly restore circulating volume with warmed blood products when hemorrhage is present (or isotonic crystalloid while blood is arranged when appropriate). Monitor mental status, pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, urine output, lactate, and response to treatment; identify and treat the source, prevent hypothermia, and escalate to surgery or interventional control of bleeding when indicated.

### Q5: A 50-year-old female presents with diffuse redness, swelling, and warmth in the right leg. She reports fever and pain. There is history of minor trauma and type I! diabetes mellitus. What is the most likely diagnosis? What are the common causative organisms? How would you manage this patient?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** The likely diagnosis is acute cellulitis of the right leg, with possible abscess or deeper infection to exclude. Common organisms are beta-hemolytic streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus, including MRSA where relevant. Assess vital signs, sepsis, extent, skin breaks, pulses, sensation, and glucose; look for fluctuance, crepitus, pain out of proportion, or systemic toxicity. Mark the erythema, elevate the limb, provide analgesia and fluids as needed, optimize diabetes control, and start antibiotics covering streptococci and staphylococci according to severity and local guidance; obtain cultures in severe or purulent disease. Drain any abscess, admit for systemic illness or rapid progression, and urgently explore suspected necrotizing infection.

### Q6: What are differential diagnoses of lateral Neck swelling and management of one of them?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** Differential diagnoses of a lateral neck swelling include reactive or metastatic lymphadenopathy, lymphoma, branchial cleft cyst, cystic hygroma, carotid body tumor, parotid-tail lesion, lipoma, and a thyroid or salivary lesion. In an adult, a persistent unexplained mass must be assessed for malignancy. For a branchial cleft cyst, take a history and examine the oral cavity, pharynx, thyroid, and neck; use neck ultrasound and fine-needle aspiration, with CT or MRI when needed to define anatomy and exclude a primary malignancy. Treat any acute infection first, then excise the cyst and tract completely after diagnostic assessment.

### Q8: What are differential diagnosis of painful scrotal swelling, mention clinical picture, investigation and treatment of one of them? 1

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 1
**EXP:** Differential diagnoses of painful scrotal swelling include testicular torsion, epididymo-orchitis, torsion of a testicular appendage, incarcerated or strangulated inguinal hernia, trauma, and less commonly tumor with hemorrhage. Testicular torsion typically causes sudden severe unilateral pain, nausea or vomiting, a high-riding transverse testis, and an absent cremasteric reflex; fever and urinary symptoms are usually absent. Perform urgent examination and urinalysis; Doppler ultrasound may help only if it does not delay treatment. Suspected torsion requires immediate scrotal exploration, detorsion, and bilateral orchiopexy; orchiectomy is required if the testis is nonviable.

### Q9: 22 years old female accidentally discovered painless solitary lump at the upper lateral quadrant of her left breast, her history is irrelevant, on examination it was found firm, smooth and freely mobile. -how to investigate such lump? -what is your differential diagnosis? -what is your management based on the most possible diagnosis?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 2
**EXP:** The most likely diagnosis is a fibroadenoma, but malignancy must be excluded by triple assessment: history and examination, age-appropriate imaging (ultrasound is usually first-line at age 22), and image-guided core biopsy if imaging or clinical findings are suspicious or the lesion is enlarging. Differential diagnoses include a breast cyst, phyllodes tumor, fibrocystic change, and (rarely at this age) breast carcinoma. **Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** derived
**Source Pages:** 2, 3
**EXP:** The most likely diagnosis is an irreducible obstructed inguinoscrotal hernia with suspected strangulation, given the recent irreducibility, vomiting, and new pain. Differential diagnoses include a hydrocele, testicular or epididymal mass, varicocele, and an acute scrotal condition; an incarcerated femoral hernia is another possible cause of bowel obstruction. Complications include intestinal obstruction, strangulation with bowel ischemia or gangrene, perforation, peritonitis, sepsis, and testicular ischemia. Resuscitate urgently: assess airway, breathing, and circulation; keep the patient nil by mouth; give oxygen as indicated; establish two large-bore IV lines; send blood count, electrolytes, renal function, lactate, and group and cross-match; give warmed IV fluids, analgesia, and nasogastric decompression for vomiting or obstruction; monitor vital signs and urine output with a catheter. Give perioperative broad-spectrum antibiotics and obtain urgent surgical review. Do not delay operation for imaging when strangulation is suspected. Perform emergency exploration, reduce the hernia, assess bowel viability, resect nonviable bowel with appropriate anastomosis, and repair the hernia; avoid mesh if the operative field is contaminated or infected.

### Q7: What are the circulatory changes that occur after birth? Describe the different stages of development of the interatrial and interventricular septa. What type of tissue is critical for dividing the heart into four chambers and the outflow tract into pulmonary and aortic channels?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** Postnatal changes of the fetal circulation are caused by cessation of placental blood flow and the beginning of respiration; the change from the fetal to the adult pattern is not a sudden event — some changes occur with the first breath, others take hours, days or months, in this order: (1) bradykinin released from the lungs with initial inflation closes the ductus arteriosus by contraction of its walls, with complete anatomical closure (to the ligamentum arteriosum) over 1-3 months; (2) closure of the foramen ovale is caused by increased left-atrial pressure combined with decreased right-atrial pressure — the first breath pushes the septum primum against the septum secundum, this closure is reversible for the first days of life (crying causes a right-to-left shunt, accounting for neonatal cyanotic periods), and constant apposition gradually fuses the two septa over about a year to form the fossa ovalis; (3) the umbilical arteries close by muscular contraction within minutes of birth (obliteration over 2-3 months), forming the medial umbilical ligaments; (4) the umbilical vein closes shortly after, forming the ligamentum teres of the liver; (5) the ductus venosus closes to form the ligamentum venosum of the liver. During atrial septation, the septum primum grows from the roof of the atrium toward the fusing endocardial cushions during the 4th week, leaving the foramen (ostium) primum as a right-to-left shunt; before this closes, programmed cell death perforates the septum primum to form the foramen (ostium) secundum; the septum secundum then descends on the right side of the septum primum, overlapping the foramen secundum and leaving the foramen ovale, guarded by the valve of the foramen ovale (the remaining septum primum). Ventricular septation: by the end of the 4th week the two primitive ventricles expand, with continuous myocardial growth outside and trabeculation/diverticulation inside; the medial walls of the expanding ventricles appose and fuse to form the muscular interventricular septum, and an outgrowth from the inferior endocardial cushion forms the membranous part of the septum, fusing with the muscular part to close the interventricular foramen. The mesenchymal cells — derived mainly from neural crest cells — that form ridges in the common tube of the bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus are the tissue critical for dividing the heart into four chambers and the outflow tract into separate pulmonary and aortic channels
**Tag:** Department, GDs, Anatomy GD 2
**tagSuggere:** Anatomy

### Q8: A 55-year-old obese male was rushed to casualty with chest pain radiating to the left arm, sweating and severe dyspnea. He is a chronic smoker and a known hypertensive on irregular treatment for 15 years, diagnosed with left-sided heart failure with infarction. HR 120/min, BP 160/100 mmHg, RR 35/min, elevated JVP. Explain the elevated JVP in this case, and mention the significance of the other JVP curve waves

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** Normal JVP waveform: the a wave is atrial contraction, x descent is atrial relaxation, the c wave is bulging of the tricuspid valve into the atrium during isovolumic ventricular contraction, the v wave is passive atrial filling against a closed tricuspid valve, and the y descent is atrial emptying as the tricuspid valve opens. Abnormal patterns of clinical significance: absent a waves occur in atrial fibrillation; giant a waves, uniform on every beat, occur in right ventricular hypertrophy, tricuspid stenosis, pulmonary hypertension and COPD; cannon a waves, intermittent and of varying height, occur with premature atrial/junctional/ventricular beats, complete AV block and ventricular tachycardia (the atrium contracting against a closed tricuspid valve); and a giant v wave occurs in tricuspid regurgitation and in cardiac tamponade. In this patient the left ventricular infarction raises left-sided filling pressure, which is transmitted back through the pulmonary circulation to the right heart, raising central venous pressure and elevating the JVP

### Q14: A 60-year-old man presents with new shortness of breath for 24 hours and the sensation that his heart is beating too fast. He stopped smoking 10 years ago (40 pack-years), gained 40 lb, and drinks excess caffeine. HR is 130 and irregularly irregular, BP 160/96, RR 26, SpO2 90% on room air, bilateral rales, neck veins distended 10 cm above the angle of Louis, and trace bilateral leg edema. What are the predisposing factors for atrial fibrillation, and what are its mechanism and ECG changes?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** Risk factors for atrial fibrillation include a positive cardiac history, hypertension, increasing age, obesity and heavy smoking. Mechanism: atrial fibrillation occurs when the atria are stimulated to beat repeatedly and irregularly, usually at an atrial rate greater than 400 impulses/minute; it often involves micro-reentry and several ectopic foci firing independently in the atria, so atrial contractions are not synchronized (ineffective atrial contraction), and the chaotic atrial depolarization waves penetrate the AV node irregularly, producing irregular ventricular contractions. Atrial fibrillation increases the risk of stroke around 4-5 times, and this risk is increased further by age, hypertension, heart failure, diabetes and a previous history of blood clots

### Q15: Describe the difference between atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter on ECG

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key

### Q17: What is meant by a "dropped beat," and mention its types and ECG changes

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** An extrasystole (premature beat/premature contraction) is a beat that occurs before the expected next normal beat and transiently interrupts the cardiac rhythm; it is caused by an abnormal impulse from a hyperexcitable ectopic focus in the atrial or ventricular myocardium, or by reentry. There are three types. (1) Atrial extrasystole: the P wave is of abnormal shape, the QRS is normal in shape, the PR interval and the interval between the premature beat and the preceding contraction are shortened, and the interval between the premature beat and the next beat is prolonged. (2) Junctional (AV nodal) extrasystole: if the impulse reaches the atria before the ventricles there are inverted P waves with a short PR interval and slow ventricular rate; if it reaches both at the same time the P wave is absent, masked by the QRS; if it reaches the ventricles before the atria the inverted P wave falls after the QRS. (3) Ventricular extrasystole: the P wave is absent (failure of retrograde conduction from ventricle to atrium), the QRS is abnormal in shape (notched, because the two ventricles are not excited simultaneously) and prolonged (because the impulse is conducted mainly through the slowly-conducting ventricular muscle rather than the specialized conducting system), and the T wave is inverted due to a disturbance in repolarization. The strength of the premature beat depends on whether it falls early or late in the relative refractory period, and a compensatory pause typically follows
**Tag:** Department, GDs, Physiology GD 4
**tagSuggere:** Physiology

### Q18: What's meant by extrasystole, mention its types and ECG changes

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** Extrasystole (a premature beat or premature contraction) is a beat that occurs before the expected next normal beat and transiently interrupts the cardiac rhythm. There are three types — atrial extrasystole, AV nodal (junctional) extrasystole, and ventricular extrasystole, the most dangerous being the ventricular type. Atrial extrasystole: abnormally shaped P wave, normal QRS, shortened PR interval and shortened interval to the preceding beat, with a prolonged interval to the next beat. Junctional extrasystole: inverted P waves that fall before, hidden within, or after the QRS depending on the relative timing of atrial and ventricular activation, with a short PR interval when the P precedes the QRS. Ventricular extrasystole: absent P wave, an abnormal (notched, prolonged) QRS because the ventricles are not excited simultaneously through the normal conducting system, and an inverted T wave from disturbed repolarization
**Tag:** Department, GDs, Physiology GD 4
**tagSuggere:** Physiology

### Q19: The mediastinum: What are the definition and subdivisions of the mediastinum? List the contents of the middle mediastinum. Describe the pericardial sinuses. Describe the surface anatomy of the borders of the heart

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** The mediastinum is a movable central partition that separates the two laterally placed pleural cavities and lungs. It is divided into the superior mediastinum and the inferior mediastinum, separated by a transverse plane from the sternal angle to the lower border of T4; the inferior mediastinum is further subdivided by the pericardial sac into the anterior, middle and posterior mediastinum. Structures in the middle mediastinum are the heart, pericardium, roots of the great vessels, phrenic nerves, and pericardial and phrenic arteries. The transverse pericardial sinus is a short passage between the reflection of serous pericardium around the aorta and pulmonary trunk anteriorly and the reflection around the superior vena cava posteriorly; it forms as a consequence of the heart tube's developmental looping and bending. The oblique pericardial sinus lies on the posterior surface of the heart, posterior to the left atrium, allowing distension of the chamber, bounded by the reflection of serous pericardium around the pulmonary veins and the inferior vena cava. Surface anatomy of the heart's borders: the superior border extends from a point on the second left costal cartilage 1.3 cm from the sternal edge to a point on the third right costal cartilage 1.3 cm from the sternal edge; the right border extends from that point on the third right costal cartilage down to a point on the sixth right costal cartilage 1.3 cm from the sternal edge; the left border extends from the second left costal cartilage to the apex in the fifth left intercostal space, 9 cm from the midline; and the inferior border extends from the sixth right costal cartilage to that same apex

### Q20: After severe physical exercise, a 9-year-old boy began feeling unwell. He was brought to the emergency department semiconscious with an irregular heart rate. **Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key

### Q25: On examination, the upper body of a 7-year-old boy is much more developed than his lower body. Blood pressure in the upper extremities exceeds that in the lower extremities, the lower extremities are cold, and femoral pulses are absent. What is the possible congenital abnormality causing these manifestations, what are its main varieties, and what collateral circulations may help ameliorate it?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** The diagnosis is coarctation (constriction) of the aorta. Its main varieties are the postductal (adult) type, in which the constriction is distal to the ductus arteriosus, and the preductal (infantile) type, in which the constriction is proximal to the ductus arteriosus, so blood flows through the patent ductus into the descending aorta; closure of the ductus at birth in this type causes rapid deterioration, and prostaglandin infusion is given to try to reopen the ductus. The collateral circulation between the intercostal, internal thoracic and scapular arteries develops during fetal life to help pass blood to the lower body around the constriction

### Q28: A patient presents with ischemic chest discomfort. Which cardiac-marker enzymes would you order to assist in the diagnosis? Compare and contrast the enzyme pattern if the process has been going on for 3 hours, 24 hours, or 72 hours, and explain the various enzymes that might be elevated during the course of an infarction

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** The list of cardiac markers includes creatine kinase (CK), troponins, aspartate transaminase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), myoglobin, and heart fatty-acid binding protein (H-FABP). Troponin I and T are specific for cardiac injury, rise at 4-8 hours, peak at 12 hours, and return to normal after 5-10 days (troponin I) or up to 15 days (troponin T). CK-MB is specific after excluding skeletal injury or hypothyroidism, rises at 4-8 hours, peaks at 24 hours, and returns to normal in 3-4 days. CK lacks specificity and sensitivity, rises at 4-8 hours, peaks at 24 hours, and normalizes in 3-4 days. Myoglobin is sensitive and specific after excluding skeletal injury or renal failure; it rises earliest, at 1-2 hours, peaks at 6-8 hours, and returns to normal after 1 day. AST is non-specific, rising at 8-12 hours, peaking at 1-2 days, and normalizing in 3-4 days. LDH-1 and LDH-2 'flip' at 24-48 hours, peak at 3-6 days, and return to baseline in 8-14 days. So at 3 hours mainly myoglobin would be detectable; at 24 hours troponin, CK-MB and CK would all be near their peak while myoglobin is already falling; and at 72 hours troponin remains elevated while CK-MB, CK and myoglobin have largely normalized

### Q29: A young man suffers from severe headache and blurring of vision. His blood pressure is 250/160 mmHg, eye examination shows papilloedema, and urine examination shows proteinuria. What is the diagnosis, what are the causes of hypertension, and what are the causes of death in this disease?

Causes of death in malignant hypertension: acute renal failure (95%), cerebral hemorrhage, and left-sided heart failure

### Q30: A young man suffers from severe headache and blurring of vision. His blood pressure is 250/160 mmHg, eye examination shows papilloedema, and urine examination shows proteinuria. ### Q32: A 56-year-old woman arrives in the emergency department complaining of dizziness and headache. Her blood pressure is 210/140 mmHg. She is not currently taking any medications and has not seen a doctor in several years. Define arterial blood pressure and peripheral resistance and give their normal values, and explain the reflexes that control arterial blood pressure

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** Arterial blood pressure (ABP) is the force exerted by the blood stream on the lateral walls of the arteries, leading to distension; the normal value is 120/80 mmHg. Peripheral resistance (PR) is the resistance the blood meets during its passage through the peripheral arteries and capillaries; it depends on the diameter of the arteries and capillaries (vasoconstriction raises PR) and on the viscosity of blood (which depends on plasma proteins and RBC number, rising in polycythaemia and haemoconcentration). Rapidly-acting pressure control mechanisms, acting within seconds to minutes: (1) the baroreceptor feedback reflex, which corrects a drop in ABP by increasing heart rate and causing vasoconstriction; (2) the CNS ischemic mechanism — when ABP falls markedly (below 60 mmHg) and cerebral blood flow drops severely, cerebral ischemia triggers powerful vasoconstriction to restore brain perfusion (the 'last-ditch-stand' pressure control mechanism); (3) the arterial chemoreceptor mechanism — a fall in ABP below 80 mmHg causes O₂ lack and CO₂/H⁺ accumulation, stimulating the chemoreceptor reflex to raise heart rate, cause vasoconstriction and restore ABP; (4) the abdominal compression reflex, in which baroreceptor/chemoreceptor activation also causes skeletal-muscle vasoconstriction and compression of abdominal venous reservoirs to translocate blood toward the heart. Intermediate mechanisms, acting over minutes: the renin-angiotensin vasoconstrictor system, stress-relaxation of over-stretched vessel walls, and the capillary fluid shift mechanism. Longer-term mechanisms: the atrial/pulmonary-artery volume reflex (increased blood volume causes reflex renal vasodilation and natriuresis, decreased ADH secretion, and release of atrial natriuretic peptide) and the kidney-based long-term mechanism, in which excess salt intake raises blood volume and ABP, the kidney responds with pressure diuresis and natriuresis to restore ABP, while a fall in ABP stimulates renin secretion and angiotensin II formation, causing renal salt/water retention that restores blood volume and ABP

### Q33: A 17-year-old patient complains of severe headache. Blood pressure is 260/160 mmHg. Kidney function tests are abnormal, and there is narrowing of the left renal artery. **Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** The diagnosis is renal (renovascular) hypertension from left renal artery stenosis. ### Q77: What is the definition of circulatory shock? What are the major causes?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** Circulatory shock is a generalized inadequacy of blood flow and inadequate delivery of substrates and oxygen to meet the metabolic needs of the tissues, to a degree that the tissues are damaged. The major etiologic classifications are: (1) hypovolemic shock — from fluid loss (dehydration: gastroenteritis, severe vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, severe burns) or haemorrhage (trauma, surgery, gastrointestinal bleeding); (2) cardiogenic shock — a decreased contractile state causing decreased stroke volume and cardiac output and therefore decreased tissue perfusion, as in congestive heart failure and ischemic heart disease; (3) distributive (normovolemic) shock — the blood volume is normal but the capacity of the circulation is increased by massive vasodilation, including septic shock (a systemic inflammatory response to infection that increases capillary permeability, releases the vasodilator cachexin from macrophages, and decreases myocardial contractility), anaphylactic shock (a severe allergic Ag/Ab reaction causing direct vascular/cardiac damage and massive histamine release with vasodilation and increased capillary permeability), and neurogenic shock (loss of vasomotor tone throughout the body from brain damage, fever, excessive sleep loss, deep general or spinal anaesthesia, accident, severe emotion, head injury, spinal shock, or certain drugs); and (4) obstructive shock

### Q86: What is meant by compensated heart failure?

**Correct Answer:** -
**Answer Source:** key
**EXP:** When cardiac output falls, many circulatory reflexes are activated: (1) the best-known is the baroreceptor reflex, activated by diminished arterial pressure; (2) the chemoreceptor reflex; (3) the central nervous system ischemic response; (4) reflexes originating in the damaged heart that activate the sympathetic nervous system and inhibit parasympathetic supply; (5) sympathetic stimulation strengthens the damaged musculature and increases venous return by increasing the tone of blood vessels, especially the veins; (6) chronic compensation occurs through renal retention of fluid. In compensated heart failure, any attempt at heavy exercise usually causes immediate acute failure, because the heart cannot increase its pumping capacity to the level exercise requires — so the cardiac reserve is said to be reduced in compensated heart failure
